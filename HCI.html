        Diwali, the Festival of Lights

        Observed: October / November

        Celebrated at night

        शुभ दीपावली Shubh Deepavali, “Happy Diwali”

        Diwali symbolizes the spiritual victory of light over darkness and good over evil. Families light traditional oil lamps (diyas) and create intricate floor patterns.

        Read more about Diwali

        What it celebrates

        Diwali, or Deepavali, means “row of lights”. For many Hindus it marks the return of Lord Rama to Ayodhya after defeating Ravana, and it honours Lakshmi, the goddess of prosperity. Jains mark the nirvana of Mahavira, Sikhs celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas, and some Buddhists observe it too.

        How it’s celebrated

        In much of India it runs over five days. Families clean and decorate their homes, wear new clothes, draw rangoli patterns at the door, light rows of clay lamps (diyas), pray to Lakshmi on the main night, and visit relatives with gifts. Fireworks light up the sky in many towns.

        Food

        Sweets (mithai) are shared with family, friends and neighbours: laddoos, barfi, kaju katli, jalebi and gulab jamun, along with savoury snacks such as chakli and namak para.

        When

        On the new moon of the Hindu month of Kartika, so the date moves each year; the main day can differ by one day between regions.

        • 2026: 8 November
        • 2027: 29 October
        • 2028: 17 October

        Where

        Across India and Nepal (where it is called Tihar), and in Sri Lanka, Singapore, Malaysia, Mauritius, Fiji, Trinidad and Tobago, Guyana and Suriname, as well as in Indian communities around the world.

        Lunar New Year

        Observed: January / February

        Celebrated at night

        新年快乐 Xīnnián kuàilè, “Happy New Year”

        Marking the beginning of a new calendar cycle based on lunar phases, this festival features glowing red lanterns, energetic dragon dances, and family reunion dinners.

        Read more about Lunar New Year

        What it celebrates

        The first day of the year in the Chinese lunisolar calendar, also called the Spring Festival (春节, Chūnjié). It is a time to see out the old year, welcome luck for the new one, honour ancestors and, above all, bring the family back together. Each year is linked to one of the twelve animals of the zodiac.

        How it’s celebrated

        Before the new year, homes are swept clean to clear away bad luck and decorated with red lanterns, paper cuttings and couplets on the doors. On New Year’s Eve families gather for a reunion dinner, often after long journeys home. Elders give red envelopes of money (hóngbāo) to children, firecrackers and fireworks scare off bad luck, and lion and dragon dances fill the streets. The celebrations last fifteen days and end with the Lantern Festival.

        Food

        Dishes are chosen for their lucky meanings: a whole fish, as “fish” (yú) sounds like “surplus”; dumplings (jiǎozi), shaped like old silver ingots, especially in the north; sticky rice cake (niángāo) for a “higher year”; and sweet rice balls (tāngyuán) at the Lantern Festival.

        When

        On the second new moon after the winter solstice, so it falls between 21 January and 20 February.

        • 2027: 6 February
        • 2028: 26 January
        • 2029: 13 February

        Where

        Across China, Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan, and in Singapore, Malaysia and Indonesia. Vietnam celebrates Tết and Korea celebrates Seollal on or around the same day, and Chinese communities around the world mark it with parades and festivals.

        Sources

        Lunar New Year on Wikipedia

        Eid al-Fitr

        Observed: Varies (Lunar Calendar)

        Celebrated by day

        عيد مبارك Eid Mubarak, “Blessed Eid”

        Marking the conclusion of Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr is a celebration focused on morning prayers, acts of charity, gift-giving, and festive communal meals. It is celebrated by Muslims worldwide.

        Read more about Eid al-Fitr

        What it celebrates

        Eid al-Fitr, the “Festival of Breaking the Fast”, marks the end of Ramadan, the month in which Muslims fast from dawn to sunset. It is a day of thanks to God for the strength to complete the fast, and of generosity, forgiveness and togetherness.

        How it’s celebrated

        The day begins with a special congregational prayer, held in mosques and open grounds shortly after sunrise. Before the prayer, Muslims give zakat al-fitr, a gift of food or money so that people in need can celebrate too. Families dress in new or their best clothes, visit relatives and friends, and children often receive gifts or money. In many countries the holiday lasts one to three days; in Indonesia, millions travel home for Lebaran.

        Food

        Sweets are so central that in Turkey the holiday is also called Şeker Bayramı, the “Sugar Festival”. Favourites include date-filled maamoul in the Levant and the Gulf, kahk biscuits in Egypt, sheer khurma (vermicelli cooked with milk and dates) in South Asia, and ketupat rice cakes with rendang in Indonesia and Malaysia.

        When

        On the first day of Shawwal, the tenth month of the Islamic lunar calendar. It begins when the new crescent moon is sighted, so the date can differ by a day between countries, and it moves about eleven days earlier each year. Expected dates (Umm al-Qura calendar):

        • 2027: about 9 March
        • 2028: about 26 February
        • 2029: about 14 February

        Where

        Muslims around the world, with public holidays across the Middle East, North Africa, Central, South and Southeast Asia. It is called Hari Raya Aidilfitri in Malaysia and Singapore and Lebaran in Indonesia, the country with the most Muslims.

        Rio de Janeiro Carnival

        Observed: February / March

        Celebrated at night

        Bom Carnaval! “Have a good Carnival”

        Considered the largest carnival in the world, this Brazilian celebration features vibrant Samba parades, extravagant costumes, lively street music, and dancing.

        Read more about Rio Carnival

        What it celebrates

        Carnival is the last celebration before Lent, the forty days of fasting before Easter in the Christian calendar. In Rio de Janeiro it grew into a celebration of samba, the music and dance born in the city’s Afro-Brazilian communities.

        How it’s celebrated

        Rio’s samba schools (escolas de samba), each rooted in a neighbourhood, spend the whole year preparing. In the Sambadrome, designed by Oscar Niemeyer, each school parades for over an hour with thousands of dancers in costume, a drum section (bateria) and huge floats, telling a theme through its own samba song, and judges pick a champion. Across the city, hundreds of free street parties (blocos) fill the streets with music from morning to night.

        Food

        Carnival runs on street food: pastéis (fried pastries), grilled skewers, açaí and cold coconut water. Feijoada, Brazil’s black-bean and pork stew, is a favourite at samba-school gatherings.

        When

        From the Friday before Ash Wednesday to the Tuesday, so it moves with Easter and falls between early February and early March. The top twelve samba schools parade in the Sambadrome over the Sunday, Monday and Tuesday nights.

        • 2027: 5–9 February
        • 2028: 25–29 February
        • 2029: 9–13 February

        Where

        Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. Other Brazilian cities have famous carnivals of their own, such as Salvador, with music trucks called trios elétricos, and Recife and Olinda, with frevo dancing.

        Oktoberfest

        Observed: September / October

        Celebrated by day

        O’zapft is! “It’s tapped!, the cry that opens the first barrel”

        Held annually in Munich, Germany, Oktoberfest is the world's largest Volksfest, featuring traditional Bavarian music, local foods, and cultural heritage events.

        Read more about Oktoberfest

        What it celebrates

        Oktoberfest began with the wedding of Crown Prince Ludwig of Bavaria and Princess Therese of Saxe-Hildburghausen on 12 October 1810. The people of Munich were invited to a horse race on a meadow named Theresienwiese after the bride, and the party became a yearly celebration of Bavarian culture.

        How it’s celebrated

        At noon on the first Saturday, the Mayor of Munich taps the first keg and cries “O’zapft is!”. The festival opens with a parade of the tent hosts and breweries, followed on Sunday by a parade of traditional costume groups and riflemen’s clubs. Visitors fill large beer tents run by Munich’s breweries, with brass bands, as well as fairground rides, and many wear Tracht: lederhosen and dirndls. About six million people come each year.

        Food

        Roast chicken (Hendl), pork knuckle, sausages, giant pretzels, Obatzda cheese spread, fish grilled on a stick (Steckerlfisch) and sugared roasted almonds. Beer from Munich’s breweries is served in one-litre mugs (Maß).

        When

        For about sixteen days, from a Saturday in mid or late September to the first Sunday in October. When that Sunday falls before 3 October, the festival runs on to German Unity Day on the 3rd.

        • 2027: 18 September – 3 October
        • 2028: 16 September – 3 October
        • 2029: 22 September – 7 October

        Where

        On the Theresienwiese in Munich, Bavaria, Germany. Oktoberfests are held around the world, among the largest in Kitchener-Waterloo, Canada, and Blumenau, Brazil.

        Sources

        Oktoberfest on Wikipedia

        Holi, the Festival of Colours

        Observed: March

        Celebrated by day

        होली की शुभकामनाएँ Holi ki shubhkamnayein, “Best wishes for Holi”

        Holi welcomes spring and celebrates the triumph of good over evil. People throw coloured powder and water at friends and strangers alike, share sweets, and dance in the streets.

        Read more about Holi

        What it celebrates

        Holi is the Hindu festival of spring and colours. It celebrates the end of winter and the triumph of good over evil, remembering how the young devotee Prahlad was saved from the fire when his aunt Holika tried to burn him. It also recalls the playful love of Krishna and Radha.

        How it’s celebrated

        On the first evening, Holika Dahan, bonfires are lit to burn away evil. The next morning people chase each other through the streets with coloured powder (gulal) and coloured water, with water pistols and balloons, singing and dancing to drums; everyday social rules are relaxed. In the afternoon people wash, dress up and visit friends and family. In the Braj region around Mathura and Vrindavan, linked to Krishna, celebrations go on for more than a week.

        Food

        Gujiya, fried pastries filled with sweetened milk solids and dried fruit, are the classic Holi sweet, alongside malpua (sweet pancakes), dahi vada (lentil dumplings in yoghurt) and thandai, a chilled milk drink spiced with almonds, fennel and cardamom.

        When

        On the full moon of the Hindu month of Phalguna, in late February or March, with the bonfire the evening before. Regional calendars can differ by a day. Dates of the colour day:

        • 2027: 22 March
        • 2028: 11 March
        • 2029: 28 February

        Where

        Across India and Nepal, and in Indian communities around the world, including Mauritius, Fiji and the UK. In Guyana, Trinidad and Tobago and Suriname it is called Phagwah.

        Sources

        Holi on Wikipedia

        Songkran

        Observed: 13–15 April

        Celebrated by day

        สุขสันต์วันสงกรานต์ Suk san wan Songkran, “Happy Songkran”

        The Thai New Year is a time to visit family and temples and to pour scented water over elders’ hands for blessings. In the streets it becomes a nationwide water fight.

        Read more about Songkran

        What it celebrates

        Songkran is the traditional Thai New Year, when the sun passes into Aries; the name comes from the Sanskrit saṅkrānti, “passing”. It is a time of cleansing, making merit and honouring elders, and UNESCO lists it as Intangible Cultural Heritage.

        How it’s celebrated

        Families clean their homes, visit temples to give food to monks, build small sand stupas in temple grounds and pour scented water over Buddha images. In the ceremony of rot nam dam hua, younger people pour water over their elders’ hands to ask for blessings. In the streets, the water turns into a nationwide water fight with buckets and water guns, and people dab each other’s cheeks with white clay paste. Chiang Mai is especially famous for it.

        Food

        Khao chae, rice served in chilled jasmine-scented water with small side dishes, is the classic dish of the hot season and Songkran, along with Thai sweets shared with family and given to monks.

        When

        Every year on 13 to 15 April, a national holiday in Thailand.

        • 2027: 13–15 April
        • 2028: 13–15 April
        • 2029: 13–15 April

        Where

        Across Thailand. Neighbouring countries celebrate their New Year at the same time: Pi Mai in Laos, Thingyan in Myanmar and Choul Chnam Thmey in Cambodia, as do the Dai people of Yunnan in China.

        Sources

        Songkran on Wikipedia

        Día de Muertos

        Observed: 1–2 November

        Celebrated at night

        ¡Feliz Día de Muertos! “Happy Day of the Dead”

        Families welcome back the souls of loved ones with ofrendas, home altars of marigolds, candles, photos and favourite foods, and keep candlelit vigils in cemeteries.

        Read more about Día de Muertos

        What it celebrates

        Día de Muertos, the Day of the Dead, is a time when families believe the souls of their loved ones return to visit. Rather than a time of mourning, it is a joyful remembrance. It blends Indigenous traditions of honouring the dead with the Catholic feasts of All Saints’ and All Souls’ Day, and UNESCO lists it as Intangible Cultural Heritage.

        How it’s celebrated

        Families build ofrendas, home altars with photos of the dead, candles, copal incense, papel picado and their loved ones’ favourite food and drink. Orange marigolds (cempasúchil) line paths of petals to guide the souls home. Families clean and decorate graves and keep candlelit vigils in cemeteries, and people dress as La Catrina, the elegant skeleton lady. Mexico City has held a large parade since 2016.

        Food

        Pan de muerto, a sweet bread topped with dough “bones”; sugar skulls (calaveras de azúcar) with the names of the living and the dead; tamales, mole, and hot chocolate or atole. The ofrenda also holds the dishes and drinks the dead enjoyed in life.

        When

        Every year on 1 and 2 November. The first day is often for children who have died and the second for adults; in some regions preparations begin in late October.

        • 2026: 1–2 November
        • 2027: 1–2 November
        • 2028: 1–2 November

        Where

        Throughout Mexico, with some of the best-known celebrations in Oaxaca, Michoacán (around Lake Pátzcuaro) and Mexico City, and in Mexican communities in the United States and around the world.

        Sources

        Day of the Dead on Wikipedia

        La Tomatina

        Observed: Last Wednesday of August

        Celebrated by day

        ¡Tomate, tomate! “Tomato, tomato!”, the crowd’s chant before the trucks arrive

        Every August, the small town of Buñol near Valencia hosts the world’s biggest food fight. For one hour, about 20,000 people pelt each other with ripe tomatoes until the streets run red, then the fire hoses wash the town clean.

        Read more about La Tomatina

        What it celebrates

        Nothing religious or historical: La Tomatina celebrates fun itself. It began in 1945, when a scuffle among young people at a parade of giant figures turned into a fight with tomatoes from a nearby vegetable stall. They came back with tomatoes the next year, and the custom stuck. It was banned in the early 1950s, but after a mock “tomato funeral” in 1957 it was allowed again, and in 2002 Spain named it a Fiesta of International Tourist Interest.

        How it’s celebrated

        The morning begins with the palo jabón: a ham is hung at the top of a greased pole, and people climb on each other’s shoulders to reach it while the crowd sings and is sprayed with water. Then trucks bring tonnes of over-ripe tomatoes into the narrow streets around the main square, and at the signal everyone throws. The town’s rules say to squash tomatoes before throwing them, throw nothing else, keep clear of the trucks and stop at the second signal. After about an hour, fire hoses and residents wash down the streets. Since 2013, entry has been by paid ticket, capped at about 20,000 people, to protect a town of about 9,000.

        Food

        The tomatoes are not for eating: they are over-ripe fruit grown for the fight. The festival week around it includes a paella contest, a nod to the region’s most famous dish: paella valenciana, rice cooked in a wide, shallow pan with chicken, rabbit, green beans and saffron, traditionally over a wood fire.

        When

        On the last Wednesday of August, around midday, at the end of a week of local festivities. Dates:

        • 2027: 25 August
        • 2028: 30 August
        • 2029: 29 August

        Where

        Buñol, a town about 40 km west of Valencia, Spain. Tomato fights inspired by it are now held in other countries too.

        Sources

        La Tomatina on Wikipedia

        Las Fallas

        Observed: 15–19 March

        Celebrated at night

        Bones Falles! “Happy Fallas!” in Valencian

        Each March, Valencia fills its squares with hundreds of giant satirical figures, built over a year by neighbourhood groups. Days of fireworks follow, and on the night of 19 March almost all of them are set on fire.

        Read more about Las Fallas

        What it celebrates

        The coming of spring and Saint Joseph, patron saint of carpenters, whose feast day is 19 March. One account traces the festival to Valencia’s carpenters burning the wooden boards and scraps they no longer needed at the end of winter. Over time the bonfires grew into elaborate monuments that poke fun at politicians, celebrities and everyday life. In 2016, UNESCO added the Valencia Fallas to its list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

        How it’s celebrated

        Each neighbourhood association (casal faller) spends the year building a falla, a monument of wood, cardboard and papier-mâché crowded with figures called ninots. They are set up in the streets by the morning of 16 March. Every day from 1 to 19 March at 2 pm, the mascletà, a deafening, rhythmic barrage of firecrackers, shakes the Plaza del Ayuntamiento. On 17 and 18 March, falleras and falleros in traditional dress carry flowers to the Virgin of the Forsaken (Virgen de los Desamparados), and the flowers are woven into a giant mantle for her statue. On the night of 19 March, the cremà: the fallas are burned, the children’s first and the City Hall’s last, while firefighters wet the nearby buildings and keep the crowds behind barriers. One ninot each year is spared by public vote (ninot indultat) and kept in the Fallas Museum.

        Food

        Stalls all over the city fry bunyols (buñuelos), soft fritters often made with pumpkin, which people dip in thick hot chocolate, along with churros and roasted chestnuts. Neighbourhood associations cook huge paellas in the street for their members.

        When

        The main days are 15 to 19 March every year, with the daily mascletà from 1 March and the opening call (Crida) on the last Sunday of February. Main days:

        • 2027: 15–19 March
        • 2028: 15–19 March
        • 2029: 15–19 March

        Where

        Valencia, Spain, and many other towns of the Valencian region, which hold fallas of their own.

        Sources

        Falles on Wikipedia; Valencia Fallas festivity, UNESCO

        Venice Carnival

        Observed: February / March (the two weeks before Lent)

        Celebrated by day

        A Carnevale ogni scherzo vale “At Carnival, any prank goes”, an old Italian saying

        In the two weeks before Lent, Venice fills with masks. People in elaborate costumes stroll the squares and bridges, as the city brings back the carnival of the old Venetian Republic.

        Read more about Venice Carnival

        What it celebrates

        Like other carnivals, it is a last burst of feasting and fun before Lent, the forty days of fasting before Easter in the Christian calendar. Venice’s carnival goes back to the Middle Ages; legend links it to the Republic of Venice’s victory over the Patriarch of Aquileia in 1162. For centuries masks let Venetians of every class mix in the streets as equals. The festival was banned in 1797, when the Republic fell, and was revived in 1979.

        How it’s celebrated

        Masqueraders in silk, lace and feathers gather in Piazza San Marco and along the canals, posing for photographs without a word. Traditional masks include the bauta, which covers the whole face, the half-mask colombina, the plain white volto and the beaked plague doctor (medico della peste). The opening weekend’s Flight of the Angel (Volo dell’Angelo) sends a performer down a wire from the bell tower of San Marco to the square, and judges pick the most beautiful mask. Palaces host masked balls and dinners, and about three million visitors come each year.

        Food

        Frittelle (fritole in Venetian) are the Carnival sweet: fried balls of dough with raisins and pine nuts, or filled with custard or zabaglione and rolled in sugar. Galani, thin ribbons of fried pastry dusted with icing sugar, are found in every bakery.

        When

        It ends on Shrove Tuesday (Martedì Grasso), the day before Ash Wednesday, so it moves with Easter. It starts about two weeks before; the city publishes the programme each autumn. Last day:

        • 2027: 9 February (from about 30 January)
        • 2028: 29 February
        • 2029: 13 February

        Where

        Venice, Italy, mostly around Piazza San Marco. Italy’s other famous carnivals include Viareggio’s papier-mâché floats and Ivrea’s Battle of the Oranges.

        Sources

        Carnival of Venice on Wikipedia; Official site of the Carnevale di Venezia

        Bastille Day, France’s national day

        Observed: 14 July

        Celebrated by day

        Bonne fête nationale ! “Happy national day!”

        France’s national day, the 14th of July, remembers the storming of the Bastille in 1789 and the festival of national unity a year later. Paris holds a military parade on the Champs-Élysées, and towns across the country end the day with fireworks and dances.

        Read more about Bastille Day

        What it celebrates

        On 14 July 1789, crowds in Paris stormed the Bastille, a royal fortress and prison that stood for the power of the king, in the first days of the French Revolution. A year later, on 14 July 1790, the Fête de la Fédération on the Champ de Mars celebrated the unity of the French nation. In 1880 the Third Republic made 14 July the national holiday, recalling both events. In French it is simply the Fête nationale or le 14 Juillet; “Bastille Day” is the English name.

        How it’s celebrated

        In the morning, a military parade goes down the Champs-Élysées in front of the President, as it has every year since 1880 (on the Champs-Élysées since 1918), while the jets of the Patrouille de France trail blue, white and red smoke overhead. Towns and villages hold their own parades, concerts and fireworks; in Paris, a concert and fireworks at the Eiffel Tower close the day. Around the 13th and 14th, many fire stations open their courtyards for public dances, the bals des pompiers. For many people it is above all a summer day off with family and friends.

        Food

        There is no special dish for the 14th of July. People gather for picnics in parks and on riverbanks, family lunches and barbecues, and town squares serve drinks and snacks at the evening dances.

        When

        On 14 July every year, a public holiday across France.

        • 2027: Wednesday 14 July
        • 2028: Friday 14 July
        • 2029: Saturday 14 July

        Where

        Across France and its overseas regions. French communities abroad celebrate too, as do places with French ties such as Pondicherry in India.

        Sources

        Bastille Day on Wikipedia

        Mardi Gras in New Orleans

        Observed: February / March

        Celebrated by day

        Laissez les bons temps rouler ! “Let the good times roll!”, the Louisiana French motto of Carnival

        New Orleans’ Carnival season builds for weeks to Mardi Gras, “Fat Tuesday”, the day before Lent. Clubs called krewes roll out parades of floats and marching bands, and riders throw beads to crowds that line the streets.

        Read more about Mardi Gras

        What it celebrates

        Mardi Gras is French for “Fat Tuesday”, the last day of feasting before Lent in the Christian calendar. French settlers brought the custom to Louisiana: in 1699 the explorers Iberville and Bienville named a spot on the Mississippi Point du Mardi Gras. Today it is a celebration of New Orleans itself, its music, food and neighbourhoods, for people of every faith, and a legal holiday in the city.

        How it’s celebrated

        The season opens on Twelfth Night, 6 January, and the parades fill its last two weeks. Krewes, private clubs, build themed floats and ride them in masks, throwing beads, doubloons and cups to people shouting “Throw me something, mister!”. Night parades are lit by flambeau carriers, a role first held by enslaved and free Black men. On Mardi Gras morning the Zulu Social Aid and Pleasure Club, founded by Black New Orleanians, parades with its prized hand-painted coconuts, followed by Rex, the “King of Carnival”, whose colours of purple, green and gold (justice, faith and power) have marked the season since 1872. In Black neighbourhoods, the Mardi Gras Indians, also called Black Masking Indians, come out in beaded and feathered suits they spend all year sewing by hand. About 1.4 million visitors come.

        Food

        King cake, a ring of sweet dough iced in purple, green and gold, is eaten from Twelfth Night to Mardi Gras. A tiny plastic baby is hidden inside, and whoever finds it brings the next cake. Parade crowds eat po’boys, fried chicken, gumbo and jambalaya.

        When

        Mardi Gras falls 47 days before Easter, on a Tuesday between 3 February and 9 March; the biggest parades run in the twelve days before it. Mardi Gras Day:

        • 2027: 9 February
        • 2028: 29 February
        • 2029: 13 February

        Where

        New Orleans, Louisiana, USA, and along the Gulf Coast; Mobile, Alabama, says its celebration is the oldest in the country. In rural Cajun Louisiana, riders on horseback go farm to farm in the Courir de Mardi Gras.

        Sources

        Mardi Gras in New Orleans on Wikipedia; King cake on Wikipedia

        Coachella Valley Music and Arts Festival

        Observed: Two weekends in April

        Celebrated at night

        Coachella is a modern music festival rather than a tradition. Since 1999 it has filled a polo club in the California desert, now for two April weekends, with big-name concerts, giant art installations and about 250,000 visitors.

        Read more about Coachella

        What it celebrates

        Music and art, not a religious or historical event. Coachella is a commercial festival run by the concert promoter Goldenvoice, part of AEG, and first held over two days in October 1999. It became one of the biggest and highest-earning music festivals in the world, and its line-ups, shows and fashion are followed online far beyond California. It belongs on a festival atlas as a newer kind of festival: a ticketed event that many people plan their year around.

        How it’s celebrated

        Each weekend runs from Friday to Sunday with the same line-up. Stages and tents named after deserts, such as the Sahara tent for electronic music, run from midday until after midnight. Huge art installations, many lit up at night, stand on the lawns beside a Ferris wheel, and many visitors camp on site. Since 2011 sets have been streamed live online. Tickets are expensive, starting at about 550 US dollars for 2027, and the festival has drawn criticism over its cost and over visitors wearing Native American–style headdresses.

        Food

        Food comes from restaurant and street-food stalls across the grounds. The Coachella Valley around it grows most of the dates in the United States, and a date shake, a milkshake blended with dates, is the local speciality.

        When

        Two weekends in April, usually the second and third. Dates are announced about a year ahead:

        • 2027: 9–11 and 16–18 April
        • 2028: not yet announced
        • 2029: not yet announced

        Where

        The Empire Polo Club in Indio, California, USA. The same grounds host Stagecoach, a country-music festival, the following weekend.

        Sources

        Coachella on Wikipedia; Official site

        Viña del Mar International Song Festival

        Observed: Six nights in late February

        Celebrated at night

        ¡El Monstruo! “The Monster”, the nickname of the festival’s famously demanding crowd

        Latin America’s oldest and best-known song festival: since 1960, six summer nights in Viña del Mar’s open-air amphitheatre, broadcast live, where a famously demanding crowd decides who goes home with a silver seagull.

        Read more about Viña del Mar Festival

        What it celebrates

        Popular music, from Chilean folk to Latin pop, rock, reggaeton and comedy. The festival is an event, not a tradition with religious or historical roots: the city of Viña del Mar founded it in 1960 as a song contest to bring summer visitors to its seaside. It grew into one of the biggest music shows in the Spanish-speaking world, and a good night there can launch a career across Latin America.

        How it’s celebrated

        Each night, about 15,000 people fill the amphitheatre of the Quinta Vergara park for concerts by international stars and Chilean acts, stand-up comedians and an international and a folk song contest. The audience, nicknamed el Monstruo (“the Monster”), is famous for its verdicts: it chants for performers it loves to receive the Gaviota de Plata (silver seagull) or Gaviota de Oro (gold), and boos those it does not, sometimes off the stage. The show is broadcast live on television and online to an audience across the Americas and beyond, and the week opens with a red-carpet gala.

        Food

        It is the height of Chile’s summer, so the classic treat is mote con huesillo, a cold drink of dried peaches in syrup with husked wheat, sold at stalls on the coast, along with empanadas de pino, baked pastries filled with beef, onion, egg and olive.

        When

        Six nights in the second half of February, with the dates set by the organisers each year:

        • 2027: 22–27 February (gala on 19 February)
        • 2028: not yet announced
        • 2029: not yet announced

        Where

        The Quinta Vergara amphitheatre in Viña del Mar, a seaside city next to Valparaíso, Chile.

        Guelaguetza

        Observed: The two Mondays after 16 July

        Celebrated by day

        ¡Viva Oaxaca! “Long live Oaxaca!”

        Each July, the city of Oaxaca hosts the Guelaguetza. Delegations from all over the state, many of them Indigenous communities, share their dances, music and dress on the hill of El Fortín, and dancers end by sharing gifts from their region with the audience.

        Read more about Guelaguetza

        What it celebrates

        The many cultures of the state of Oaxaca, one of Mexico’s most Indigenous regions, home to Zapotec, Mixtec, Mazatec, Chinantec, Mixe and other peoples. The word comes from Zapotec and names a custom of reciprocity: giving gifts and help, such as corn or work for a wedding, that will be returned one day. The festival is tied to the feast of the Virgin of Carmen on 16 July and is often linked to older maize celebrations for the goddess Centéotl, though there is no firm evidence of a pre-Hispanic origin. Its modern form dates from 1932, when the city marked its 400th anniversary.

        How it’s celebrated

        On the Saturday before, delegations from Oaxaca’s eight regions parade through the city with their bands. On each of the two Mondays, called the Lunes del Cerro (“Mondays on the Hill”), they perform in the open-air Guelaguetza auditorium on the Cerro del Fortín, in the morning and again in the afternoon. Each region brings its own dances: in Flor de Piña, from Tuxtepec, women dance with pineapples on their shoulders, and the Danza de la Pluma, danced by men in tall feathered headdresses, closes the festival. A young woman chosen to represent Centéotl, for her knowledge of her community’s traditions, presides. Seats in the front sections are ticketed, and some communities have criticised the festival for turning traditions into a show for tourists; in 2006, protesters held a free “People’s Guelaguetza” of their own.

        Food

        Oaxaca is one of Mexico’s great food regions: mole negro, a dark sauce of chiles, chocolate and spices; tlayudas, large crisp tortillas with beans, quesillo cheese and meat; tamales; chocolate and tejate, a cold drink of maize and cacao; and mezcal, with a mezcal fair held in the city at the same time.

        When

        On the two Mondays after 16 July. When the first would fall on 18 July, the anniversary of Benito Juárez’s death, both move a week later. Dates:

        • 2027: 19 and 26 July
        • 2028: 17 and 24 July
        • 2029: 23 and 30 July

        Where

        Oaxaca de Juárez, the capital of the state of Oaxaca in southern Mexico. Oaxacan communities in the United States, such as in Los Angeles, hold Guelaguetzas of their own.

        Sources

        Guelaguetza on Wikipedia; Guelaguetza on Spanish Wikipedia

        Festa de Iemanjá

        Observed: 2 February

        Celebrated by day

        Odoyá! the greeting to Iemanjá, often translated as “mother of the waters”

        On 2 February, crowds dressed in white and blue gather on the beach of Rio Vermelho in Salvador to honour Iemanjá, the Afro-Brazilian orixá of the sea, with flowers and gifts that fishing boats carry out to the ocean.

        Read more about Festa de Iemanjá

        What it celebrates

        Iemanjá (Yemọja in Yoruba) is an orixá, a divinity of Candomblé, the Afro-Brazilian religion rooted in the faith of Yoruba people brought to Brazil in slavery; she is also honoured in Umbanda. She is the mother of the waters and queen of the sea, protector of fishermen, mothers and families. In Salvador the festival grew from the offerings of the Rio Vermelho fishermen, who began taking their gifts to the sea there in 1924. Many Catholics and people of no particular faith join in too; 2 February is also the Catholic feast of Our Lady of Candles (Nossa Senhora das Candeias).

        How it’s celebrated

        From before dawn, people queue at the fishermen’s house on the beach to leave offerings for Iemanjá: flowers, often white roses, perfume and handwritten wishes, gathered in large woven baskets. Candomblé priestesses and their communities (terreiros) sing and dance to drums, and many people dress in white and blue, her colours. In the late afternoon, about 300 boats led by the fishermen carry the baskets and a main gift, prepared by a terreiro, a few miles out to sea and give them to the water. Since the 2000s, a campaign has asked for only biodegradable gifts, such as flowers and fruit, instead of mirrors, plastic and bottles. Afterwards, Rio Vermelho’s streets turn into a big party. For many it is a religious day, so visitors are asked not to interrupt ceremonies.

        Food

        Bahia’s cooking carries strong African roots. Acarajé, black-eyed-pea fritters fried in dendê palm oil and filled with spicy shrimp and vatapá, is sold by baianas de acarajé in white dress; it is also a sacred food in Candomblé, and the baianas’ craft is protected as part of Brazil’s heritage. Moqueca baiana, a fish stew with coconut milk and dendê oil, is another favourite.

        When

        On 2 February every year.

        • 2027: Tuesday 2 February
        • 2028: Wednesday 2 February
        • 2029: Friday 2 February

        Where

        Rio Vermelho beach in Salvador, Bahia, Brazil. Iemanjá is honoured along much of Brazil’s coast on other dates too: in Rio de Janeiro, many people throw white flowers into the sea and jump seven waves on New Year’s Eve, and devotees on the São Paulo coast celebrate on 2 February and 8 December. In Uruguay, crowds gather on the beaches of Montevideo on 2 February to leave gifts for Iemanjá.

        Sources

        Festa de Iemanjá on Portuguese Wikipedia; Yemoja on Wikipedia

        Gnaoua World Music Festival

        Observed: Three days in late June

        Celebrated at night

        Every June the Atlantic port of Essaouira fills with the sound of the guembri, as masters of the Gnawa tradition share open-air stages with jazz, rock and world-music guests. Founded in 1998, it is a public music festival that carried a Moroccan spiritual music to a worldwide audience.

        Read more about Gnaoua World Music Festival

        What it celebrates

        Gnawa (also spelled Gnaoua) is a Moroccan tradition of music and spiritual practice. Its roots lie with people brought to Morocco from sub-Saharan Africa, many of them enslaved, and it blends African, Arab-Muslim and Berber elements. Gnawa masters, called maâlems, lead brotherhoods whose all-night healing ceremony, the lila (“night”), calls on ancestors and spirits with sacred songs. UNESCO added Gnawa to its list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity in 2019.

        The festival is something different from the lila. It is a public music festival, founded in 1998 in Essaouira by the Moroccan producer Neila Tazi and run by the events company A3. It puts Gnawa masters on stage with invited musicians from jazz, rock, pop and other traditions, and many of its concerts are free. The lila remains a religious and healing ritual, held in homes and in the community’s own spaces, not a show; visitors who are invited to one should treat it with respect.

        How it’s celebrated

        For three days in late June, concerts run into the night on stages around Essaouira, including the large open-air stage at Place Moulay Hassan between the old town and the harbour. A Gnawa group is led by a maâlem playing the guembri, a three-stringed bass lute, while the others answer in song and clap the qraqeb, iron castanets, as they dance. Many sets pair a maâlem with guests from far away: in 2026 the invited artists came from port cities from Lebanon to Cameroon and Brazil. Around the concerts there are talks, a forum and a music workshop run with the Berklee College of Music. Press reports speak of several hundred thousand visitors over the days.

        Food

        Essaouira is a fishing port, and its signature meal is sardines or other fresh fish grilled on charcoal at the harbour stalls. Harira, Morocco’s tomato, lentil and chickpea soup, is eaten across the country, as are tagines and mint tea.

        When

        Three days in late June, in recent years Thursday to Saturday. The dates are announced about a year ahead:

        • 2027: Thursday 24 – Saturday 26 June
        • 2028: not yet announced
        • 2029: not yet announced

        Where

        Essaouira, on Morocco’s Atlantic coast about 175 km west of Marrakech. Its walled old town and harbour are a UNESCO World Heritage Site; the festival’s stages are in and around the old town, with the main one at Place Moulay Hassan.

        Sources

        Official site; Gnawa on UNESCO’s Intangible Cultural Heritage list; Gnaoua World Music Festival on Wikipedia

        Fez Festival of World Sacred Music

        Observed: Four to nine days in late spring (dates vary)

        Celebrated at night

        Since 1994 the ancient city of Fez has hosted a festival of sacred and spiritual music from many faiths and countries. Evening concerts fill historic settings such as the Bab Makina palace gate and the Batha gardens, in a city known for its centuries of learning and Sufi tradition.

        Read more about Fez Festival of World Sacred Music

        What it celebrates

        Music from many spiritual traditions, and the idea that it can bring people of different cultures and faiths into conversation. It is a concert festival, not a religious observance. Faouzi Skali, a Moroccan writer and anthropologist, founded it in 1994, and it is run by the Fez-based Esprit de Fès Foundation (Spirit of Fez Foundation). Its programmes have often put Muslim and Christian devotional music side by side, and they draw on the city’s Andalusian, Sufi and Amazigh heritage as well as traditions from Africa, Asia and Europe.

        How it’s celebrated

        Evening concerts are the heart of it: Sufi brotherhoods, Gnawa masters, Gregorian and Byzantine chant, gospel, Indian classical musicians, jazz and many more, in settings such as the Bab Makina gate of the old palace quarter and the oak-shaded Batha gardens. Each edition has a theme (the 2026 edition, Fès et les Mâalemines, honoured master craftspeople) and often adds talks and debates. Over the years it has welcomed performers such as Ravi Shankar, Youssou N’Dour, Salif Keita, Patti Smith and Björk. There was no edition in 2020 or 2021 because of COVID-19; the 2025 edition ran 16–24 May and the 2026 edition 4–7 June.

        Food

        Fez has a name as one of Morocco’s great food cities. Its best-known dish is pastilla (b’stilla), a pie of paper-thin pastry filled with spiced chicken, almonds and eggs, or with seafood, and dusted with sugar and cinnamon. Meals begin or end with mint tea, poured from a height into small glasses.

        When

        Late May or June; the festival has also run in other months in some years. The dates are announced a few months ahead:

        • 2027: not yet announced
        • 2028: not yet announced
        • 2029: not yet announced

        Where

        Fez, in northern Morocco. The city was founded in the 9th century, and its old medina, Fes el-Bali, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Concerts take place at venues such as Bab Makina, the gate of the old royal palace complex in Fes el-Jdid, and the Batha gardens.

        Cape Town Minstrel Carnival

        Observed: 2 January (Tweede Nuwe Jaar)

        Celebrated by day

        Tweede Nuwe Jaar! “Second New Year”, the day’s name in Afrikaans

        On Tweede Nuwe Jaar, the day after New Year’s Day, troupes called klopse parade through Cape Town in satin suits and colourful umbrellas, with brass bands, banjos and ghoema drums. The carnival grew out of the day off given to enslaved people at the Cape and is a proud celebration of the city’s Cape Malay and coloured communities.

        Read more about Cape Town Minstrel Carnival

        What it celebrates

        Freedom, music and community. At the Cape, enslaved people were traditionally given the day after New Year’s Day off, and they spent it celebrating in their own way; accounts differ on why the day was granted. Slavery at the Cape was abolished in 1834, and the gatherings continued as part of the story of Cape Town’s Cape Malay and coloured communities, many of them descended from enslaved people brought from Africa, India and Southeast Asia. Troupes took up costumes and parading in the 19th century, and the music mixes Dutch, British, African, Asian and American influences, including visiting American minstrel shows. Today it is a celebration of that heritage and of the neighbourhoods the troupes come from.

        Names matter here. The Afrikaans Kaapse Klopse (“Cape troupes”) and Tweede Nuwe Jaar are what many in the community say; the official English name is the Cape Town Minstrel Carnival. During apartheid the event was commonly known by a different name that is now widely considered a racial slur, so this atlas does not use it. The word “minstrel” and the painted faces some troupe members wear echo American blackface minstrel shows, a link that people in Cape Town still discuss and debate.

        How it’s celebrated

        Troupes of up to a thousand members, each with its own colours, board, brass band and drum major, march through the city and are judged on categories such as march-past, best dress, music and drum major. Participants wear matching satin or sequinned suits, hats and bright umbrellas, play trumpets, saxophones, banjos and the ghoema, the barrel drum whose beat runs through the carnival, and sing songs in Afrikaans and English. In recent years the main parade has run through central Cape Town; for 2026 organisers asked for a new route along Somerset Road in Green Point into the stadium, and the city reviewed the application, so the route and even the day can change from year to year. Troupe competitions follow in the stadium on the days after. Tens of thousands of performers take part.

        Food

        The Cape Malay kitchen is the carnival’s flavour. Koesisters are spiced, syrup-dipped doughnuts rolled in coconut, a different pastry from the plaited koeksisters of Afrikaans cooking. Samoosas are small fried pastries of spiced mince or vegetables, and bobotie is a baked, curry-spiced mince dish under an egg custard.

        When

        2 January, the day after New Year’s Day, although the main parade is sometimes moved (in 2026 it was proposed for Monday 5 January). The parade date is announced in December:

        • 2027: Saturday 2 January (parade date to be confirmed)
        • 2028: Sunday 2 January (parade date to be confirmed)
        • 2029: Tuesday 2 January (parade date to be confirmed)

        Where

        Cape Town, South Africa. The parade has long run through the city centre and past the Bo-Kaap, the historic Cape Malay neighbourhood, to the stadium in Green Point; the troupes come from neighbourhoods across the city and the Cape Flats.

        Sources

        Kaapse Klopse on Wikipedia; City of Cape Town, 2026 route announcement; Time Out Cape Town

        Dubai Shopping Festival

        Observed: Five to six weeks, December to January

        Celebrated at night

        Dubai Shopping Festival is a retail and entertainment event rather than a tradition. Since 1996 the city has spent weeks each winter on sales, prize raffles, concerts and fireworks, drawing visitors from around the world.

        Read more about Dubai Shopping Festival

        What it celebrates

        Shopping and entertainment, not a religious or historical event. Dubai launched the festival in February 1996 to bring visitors to its shops and hotels; the idea is credited to Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum, then crown prince and now Dubai’s ruler. It is organised by Dubai Festivals and Retail Establishment, part of the city’s Department of Economy and Tourism, under the motto “One World, One Family, One Festival”. The first festival drew more than 1.5 million visitors; it has since moved to the winter and grown to run for five or six weeks across the new year. It belongs on a festival atlas as an example of a city making a festival to put itself on the map.

        How it’s celebrated

        Shops, malls and souks across Dubai offer discounts, and many give out raffle tickets with purchases for daily draws of prizes such as cars and gold. Alongside the sales are concerts, shows, family activities, street entertainment and fireworks, including a firework display on the final night. The 2026–27 edition was reported with evening shows (“DSF Nights”) and a car season among its attractions.

        Food

        Dubai’s food is as international as its visitors. Emirati dishes to try are machboos, spiced rice cooked with meat or fish; harees, slow-cooked wheat and meat; and luqaimat, sweet fried dumplings drizzled with date syrup. Guests are welcomed with Arabic coffee (gahwa) and dates.

        When

        From early December to mid-January. Dates are announced a few months ahead:

        • 2026–27: 4 December 2026 to 17 January 2027
        • 2027–28: not yet announced
        • 2028–29: not yet announced

        Where

        Dubai, United Arab Emirates, citywide: malls such as the Dubai Mall, the gold and spice souks of Deira, Dubai Creek, the beaches and shows at venues around the city. Global Village, a seasonal entertainment park that opens each autumn, is also busy during the festival.

        Nowruz

        Observed: 20 or 21 March (the spring equinox)

        Celebrated by day

        نوروز مبارک Nowruz mobārak, “Blessed Nowruz”

        Nowruz, “new day”, is the Persian New Year, marked at the spring equinox. For some 3,000 years families in Iran and many other countries have cleaned their homes, set a table of symbolic foods and spent days visiting, feasting and gathering outdoors.

        Read more about Nowruz

        What it celebrates

        The arrival of spring and the renewal of nature. Nowruz (Persian for “new day”) begins at the March equinox, and it is the first day of the Iranian calendar. Its roots lie in the ancient Persian and Zoroastrian traditions of Iran; it was celebrated at the Achaemenid court and later became the main royal holiday under the Sasanian dynasty. For most people who celebrate it today it is a secular, family and cultural festival, though it is also a holy day for Zoroastrians, Baháʼís and some Ismaili Muslims.

        It is not any one country’s festival. Iranians, Afghans, Azerbaijanis, Tajiks, Uzbeks, Turkmens, Kazakhs, Kyrgyz, Kurds and many other peoples celebrate it, from the Caucasus and Central Asia to the Balkans and South Asia, each with their own customs and names (Navruz, Newroz, Nauryz). UNESCO lists it as shared cultural heritage of Afghanistan, Azerbaijan, India, Iran, Iraq, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Pakistan, Tajikistan, Turkey, Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan, and in 2010 the United Nations General Assembly declared 21 March the International Day of Nowruz. This atlas puts the pin in Iran, where the Iranian calendar begins with Nowruz and the haft-sin table is best known, and lights up the other countries on UNESCO’s list. Kurdish communities in Iran, Iraq, Turkey and Syria celebrate Newroz too, often with large bonfires, and in some places it has also been a day for expressing Kurdish identity. Governments have at times restricted it: Kurdish Newroz gatherings in Turkey and Syria have faced bans, restrictions and mass arrests, and in 2022 Afghanistan’s Taliban government cancelled Nowruz as a public holiday, while saying people could still celebrate privately.

        How it’s celebrated

        Preparations start weeks ahead with khaneh-tekani, the “shaking of the house”: a thorough spring clean, new clothes, and sprouting wheat or lentils for the festival table. The haft-sin table holds seven items whose Persian names begin with the letter sin, each a symbol: sabzeh (sprouts) for rebirth, samanu (wheat pudding) for abundance, senjed (dried oleaster fruit) for love, sir (garlic) for health, sib (apple) for beauty, somaq (sumac) for the colour of sunrise and serkeh (vinegar) for patience. Many tables also carry a mirror, candles, painted eggs, coins, a goldfish and a book of poetry or scripture.

        On the last Wednesday evening of the year comes Chaharshanbe Suri, when people light small bonfires and jump over them, saying the old rhyme that asks the fire to take their sickness and give them its warmth. When the equinox arrives, families gather around the table, exchange gifts, often money in envelopes, called eidi, and spend the following days visiting relatives. Celebrations last thirteen days, ending with Sizdah Bedar (“getting out of the thirteenth”), when everyone leaves home for a picnic in the countryside and the sprouts from the haft-sin table are thrown into running water. Elsewhere the customs differ: Azerbaijanis have the characters Kosa and Kechal and jump over bonfires, in northern Afghanistan the Gul-e-Surkh (red flower) festival marks the new year, and across Central Asia a pot of sumalak is stirred through the night.

        Food

        On Nowruz eve Iranian families traditionally eat sabzi polo ba mahi, rice cooked with fresh herbs, with fried or baked fish; reshteh polo, rice with noodles, is also eaten for good luck in the new year. Kuku sabzi, a herb frittata rich in parsley, coriander and dill, is a staple of the table. Sweets include baklava, noon nokhodchi (chickpea-flour biscuits) and samanu, and across Central Asia and Afghanistan the sprouted-wheat pudding sumalak is slow-cooked overnight.

        When

        The moment of the March equinox. In Iran, the first day of the year is the day that holds the equinox if it comes before noon in Tehran, and the next day if it comes after. Other countries may mark 20 or 21 March, and the International Day of Nowruz is 21 March:

        • 2027: Sunday 21 March (the equinox is just before midnight on 20 March in Tehran)
        • 2028: Monday 20 March
        • 2029: Tuesday 20 March

        Where

        Iran, where the Iranian new year begins and it is the biggest holiday, and in many other countries: Afghanistan, Azerbaijan, Iraq (especially Kurdistan), Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkey, Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan make it a public holiday or celebrate it widely, as do Pakistan and India (for example in Kashmir and among the Parsis), and diaspora communities around the world. The pin sits in the middle of Iran, since Nowruz belongs to no single place.

        Sources

        Nowruz on UNESCO’s Intangible Cultural Heritage list; UN, International Day of Nowruz; Nowruz on Wikipedia; Newroz as celebrated by Kurds on Wikipedia; Arab News, 22 March 2022
