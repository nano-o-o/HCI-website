Diwali, the Festival of Lights

Observed: October / November

Celebrated at night

शुभ दीपावली Shubh Deepavali, “Happy Diwali”

Diwali symbolizes the spiritual victory of light over darkness and good over evil. Families light traditional oil lamps (diyas) and create intricate floor patterns.

Read more about Diwali

What it celebrates

Diwali, or Deepavali, means “row of lights”. For many Hindus it marks the return of Lord Rama to Ayodhya after defeating Ravana, and it honours Lakshmi, the goddess of prosperity. Jains mark the nirvana of Mahavira, Sikhs celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas, and some Buddhists observe it too.

How it’s celebrated

In much of India it runs over five days. Families clean and decorate their homes, wear new clothes, draw rangoli patterns at the door, light rows of clay lamps (diyas), pray to Lakshmi on the main night, and visit relatives with gifts. Fireworks light up the sky in many towns.

Food

Sweets (mithai) are shared with family, friends and neighbours: laddoos, barfi, kaju katli, jalebi and gulab jamun, along with savoury snacks such as chakli and namak para.

When

On the new moon of the Hindu month of Kartika, so the date moves each year; the main day can differ by one day between regions.

  • 2026: 8 November
  • 2027: 29 October
  • 2028: 17 October

Where

Across India and Nepal (where it is called Tihar), and in Sri Lanka, Singapore, Malaysia, Mauritius, Fiji, Trinidad and Tobago, Guyana and Suriname, as well as in Indian communities around the world.

Chinese New Year

Observed: January / February

Celebrated at night

新年快乐 Xīnnián kuàilè, “Happy New Year”

Marking the beginning of a new calendar cycle based on lunar phases, this festival features glowing red lanterns, energetic dragon dances, and family reunion dinners.

Read more about Chinese New Year

What it celebrates

The first day of the year in the Chinese lunisolar calendar, also called the Spring Festival (春节, Chūnjié). It is a time to see out the old year, welcome luck for the new one, honour ancestors and, above all, bring the family back together. Each year is linked to one of the twelve animals of the zodiac.

How it’s celebrated

Before the new year, homes are swept clean to clear away bad luck and decorated with red lanterns, paper cuttings and couplets on the doors. On New Year’s Eve families gather for a reunion dinner, often after long journeys home. Elders give red envelopes of money (hóngbāo) to children, firecrackers and fireworks scare off bad luck, and lion and dragon dances fill the streets. The celebrations last fifteen days and end with the Lantern Festival.

Food

Dishes are chosen for their lucky meanings: a whole fish, as “fish” (yú) sounds like “surplus”; dumplings (jiǎozi), shaped like old silver ingots, especially in the north; sticky rice cake (niángāo) for a “higher year”; and sweet rice balls (tāngyuán) at the Lantern Festival.

When

On the second new moon after the winter solstice, so it falls between 21 January and 20 February.

  • 2027: 6 February
  • 2028: 26 January
  • 2029: 13 February

Where

Across China, Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan, and in Singapore, Malaysia and Indonesia. Vietnam celebrates Tết and Korea celebrates Seollal on or around the same day, and Chinese communities around the world mark it with parades and festivals.

Eid al-Fitr

Observed: Varies (Lunar Calendar)

Celebrated by day

عيد مبارك Eid Mubarak, “Blessed Eid”

Marking the conclusion of Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr is a celebration focused on morning prayers, acts of charity, gift-giving, and festive communal meals. It is celebrated by Muslims worldwide.

Read more about Eid al-Fitr

What it celebrates

Eid al-Fitr, the “Festival of Breaking the Fast”, marks the end of Ramadan, the month in which Muslims fast from dawn to sunset. It is a day of thanks to God for the strength to complete the fast, and of generosity, forgiveness and togetherness.

How it’s celebrated

The day begins with a special congregational prayer, held in mosques and open grounds shortly after sunrise. Before the prayer, Muslims give zakat al-fitr, a gift of food or money so that people in need can celebrate too. Families dress in new or their best clothes, visit relatives and friends, and children often receive gifts or money. In many countries the holiday lasts one to three days; in Indonesia, millions travel home for Lebaran.

Food

Sweets are so central that in Turkey the holiday is also called Şeker Bayramı, the “Sugar Festival”. Favourites include date-filled maamoul in the Levant and the Gulf, kahk biscuits in Egypt, sheer khurma (vermicelli cooked with milk and dates) in South Asia, and ketupat rice cakes with rendang in Indonesia and Malaysia.

When

On the first day of Shawwal, the tenth month of the Islamic lunar calendar. It begins when the new crescent moon is sighted, so the date can differ by a day between countries, and it moves about eleven days earlier each year. Expected dates (Umm al-Qura calendar):

  • 2027: about 9 March
  • 2028: about 26 February
  • 2029: about 14 February

Where

Muslims around the world, with public holidays across the Middle East, North Africa, Central, South and Southeast Asia. It is called Hari Raya Aidilfitri in Malaysia and Singapore and Lebaran in Indonesia, the country with the most Muslims.

Rio de Janeiro Carnival

Observed: February / March

Celebrated at night

Bom Carnaval! “Have a good Carnival”

Considered the largest carnival in the world, this Brazilian celebration features vibrant Samba parades, extravagant costumes, lively street music, and dancing.

Read more about Rio Carnival

What it celebrates

Carnival is the last celebration before Lent, the forty days of fasting before Easter in the Christian calendar. In Rio de Janeiro it grew into a celebration of samba, the music and dance born in the city’s Afro-Brazilian communities.

How it’s celebrated

Rio’s samba schools (escolas de samba), each rooted in a neighbourhood, spend the whole year preparing. In the Sambadrome, designed by Oscar Niemeyer, each school parades for over an hour with thousands of dancers in costume, a drum section (bateria) and huge floats, telling a theme through its own samba song, and judges pick a champion. Across the city, hundreds of free street parties (blocos) fill the streets with music from morning to night.

Food

Carnival runs on street food: pastéis (fried pastries), grilled skewers, açaí and cold coconut water. Feijoada, Brazil’s black-bean and pork stew, is a favourite at samba-school gatherings.

When

From the Friday before Ash Wednesday to the Tuesday, so it moves with Easter and falls between early February and early March. The top twelve samba schools parade in the Sambadrome over the Sunday, Monday and Tuesday nights.

  • 2027: 5–9 February
  • 2028: 25–29 February
  • 2029: 9–13 February

Where

Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. Other Brazilian cities have famous carnivals of their own, such as Salvador, with music trucks called trios elétricos, and Recife and Olinda, with frevo dancing.

Oktoberfest

Observed: September / October

Celebrated by day

O’zapft is! “It’s tapped!, the cry that opens the first barrel”

Held annually in Munich, Germany, Oktoberfest is the world's largest Volksfest, featuring traditional Bavarian music, local foods, and cultural heritage events.

Read more about Oktoberfest

What it celebrates

Oktoberfest began with the wedding of Crown Prince Ludwig of Bavaria and Princess Therese of Saxe-Hildburghausen on 12 October 1810. The people of Munich were invited to a horse race on a meadow named Theresienwiese after the bride, and the party became a yearly celebration of Bavarian culture.

How it’s celebrated

At noon on the first Saturday, the Mayor of Munich taps the first keg and cries “O’zapft is!”. The festival opens with a parade of the tent hosts and breweries, followed on Sunday by a parade of traditional costume groups and riflemen’s clubs. Visitors fill large beer tents run by Munich’s breweries, with brass bands, as well as fairground rides, and many wear Tracht: lederhosen and dirndls. About six million people come each year.

Food

Roast chicken (Hendl), pork knuckle, sausages, giant pretzels, Obatzda cheese spread, fish grilled on a stick (Steckerlfisch) and sugared roasted almonds. Beer from Munich’s breweries is served in one-litre mugs (Maß).

When

For about sixteen days, from a Saturday in mid or late September to the first Sunday in October. When that Sunday falls before 3 October, the festival runs on to German Unity Day on the 3rd.

  • 2027: 18 September – 3 October
  • 2028: 16 September – 3 October
  • 2029: 22 September – 7 October

Where

On the Theresienwiese in Munich, Bavaria, Germany. Oktoberfests are held around the world, among the largest in Kitchener-Waterloo, Canada, and Blumenau, Brazil.

Holi, the Festival of Colours

Observed: March

Celebrated by day

होली की शुभकामनाएँ Holi ki shubhkamnayein, “Best wishes for Holi”

Holi welcomes spring and celebrates the triumph of good over evil. People throw coloured powder and water at friends and strangers alike, share sweets, and dance in the streets.

Read more about Holi

What it celebrates

Holi is the Hindu festival of spring and colours. It celebrates the end of winter and the triumph of good over evil, remembering how the young devotee Prahlad was saved from the fire when his aunt Holika tried to burn him. It also recalls the playful love of Krishna and Radha.

How it’s celebrated

On the first evening, Holika Dahan, bonfires are lit to burn away evil. The next morning people chase each other through the streets with coloured powder (gulal) and coloured water, with water pistols and balloons, singing and dancing to drums; everyday social rules are relaxed. In the afternoon people wash, dress up and visit friends and family. In the Braj region around Mathura and Vrindavan, linked to Krishna, celebrations go on for more than a week.

Food

Gujiya, fried pastries filled with sweetened milk solids and dried fruit, are the classic Holi sweet, alongside malpua (sweet pancakes), dahi vada (lentil dumplings in yoghurt) and thandai, a chilled milk drink spiced with almonds, fennel and cardamom.

When

On the full moon of the Hindu month of Phalguna, in late February or March, with the bonfire the evening before. Regional calendars can differ by a day. Dates of the colour day:

  • 2027: 22 March
  • 2028: 11 March
  • 2029: 28 February

Where

Across India and Nepal, and in Indian communities around the world, including Mauritius, Fiji and the UK. In Guyana, Trinidad and Tobago and Suriname it is called Phagwah.

Songkran

Observed: 13–15 April

Celebrated by day

สุขสันต์วันสงกรานต์ Suk san wan Songkran, “Happy Songkran”

The Thai New Year is a time to visit family and temples and to pour scented water over elders’ hands for blessings. In the streets it becomes a nationwide water fight.

Read more about Songkran

What it celebrates

Songkran is the traditional Thai New Year, when the sun passes into Aries; the name comes from the Sanskrit saṅkrānti, “passing”. It is a time of cleansing, making merit and honouring elders, and UNESCO lists it as Intangible Cultural Heritage.

How it’s celebrated

Families clean their homes, visit temples to give food to monks, build small sand stupas in temple grounds and pour scented water over Buddha images. In the ceremony of rot nam dam hua, younger people pour water over their elders’ hands to ask for blessings. In the streets, the water turns into a nationwide water fight with buckets and water guns, and people dab each other’s cheeks with white clay paste. Chiang Mai is especially famous for it.

Food

Khao chae, rice served in chilled jasmine-scented water with small side dishes, is the classic dish of the hot season and Songkran, along with Thai sweets shared with family and given to monks.

When

Every year on 13 to 15 April, a national holiday in Thailand.

  • 2027: 13–15 April
  • 2028: 13–15 April
  • 2029: 13–15 April

Where

Across Thailand. Neighbouring countries celebrate their New Year at the same time: Pi Mai in Laos, Thingyan in Myanmar and Choul Chnam Thmey in Cambodia, as do the Dai people of Yunnan in China.

Día de Muertos

Observed: 1–2 November

Celebrated at night

¡Feliz Día de Muertos! “Happy Day of the Dead”

Families welcome back the souls of loved ones with ofrendas, home altars of marigolds, candles, photos and favourite foods, and keep candlelit vigils in cemeteries.

Read more about Día de Muertos

What it celebrates

Día de Muertos, the Day of the Dead, is a time when families believe the souls of their loved ones return to visit. Rather than a time of mourning, it is a joyful remembrance. It blends Indigenous traditions of honouring the dead with the Catholic feasts of All Saints’ and All Souls’ Day, and UNESCO lists it as Intangible Cultural Heritage.

How it’s celebrated

Families build ofrendas, home altars with photos of the dead, candles, copal incense, papel picado and their loved ones’ favourite food and drink. Orange marigolds (cempasúchil) line paths of petals to guide the souls home. Families clean and decorate graves and keep candlelit vigils in cemeteries, and people dress as La Catrina, the elegant skeleton lady. Mexico City has held a large parade since 2016.

Food

Pan de muerto, a sweet bread topped with dough “bones”; sugar skulls (calaveras de azúcar) with the names of the living and the dead; tamales, mole, and hot chocolate or atole. The ofrenda also holds the dishes and drinks the dead enjoyed in life.

When

Every year on 1 and 2 November. The first day is often for children who have died and the second for adults; in some regions preparations begin in late October.

  • 2026: 1–2 November
  • 2027: 1–2 November
  • 2028: 1–2 November

Where

Throughout Mexico, with some of the best-known celebrations in Oaxaca, Michoacán (around Lake Pátzcuaro) and Mexico City, and in Mexican communities in the United States and around the world.

La Tomatina

Observed: Last Wednesday of August

Celebrated by day

¡Tomate, tomate! “Tomato, tomato!”, the crowd’s chant before the trucks arrive

Every August, the small town of Buñol near Valencia hosts the world’s biggest food fight. For one hour, about 20,000 people pelt each other with ripe tomatoes until the streets run red, then the fire hoses wash the town clean.

Read more about La Tomatina

What it celebrates

Nothing religious or historical: La Tomatina celebrates fun itself. It began in 1945, when a scuffle among young people at a parade of giant figures turned into a fight with tomatoes from a nearby vegetable stall. They came back with tomatoes the next year, and the custom stuck. It was banned in the early 1950s, but after a mock “tomato funeral” in 1957 it was allowed again, and in 2002 Spain named it a Fiesta of International Tourist Interest.

How it’s celebrated

The morning begins with the palo jabón: a ham is hung at the top of a greased pole, and people climb on each other’s shoulders to reach it while the crowd sings and is sprayed with water. Then trucks bring tonnes of over-ripe tomatoes into the narrow streets around the main square, and at the signal everyone throws.

The town’s rules say to squash tomatoes before throwing them, throw nothing else, keep clear of the trucks and stop at the second signal. After about an hour, fire hoses and residents wash down the streets. Since 2013, entry has been by paid ticket, capped at about 20,000 people, to protect a town of about 9,000.

Food

The tomatoes are not for eating: they are over-ripe fruit grown for the fight. The festival week around it includes a paella contest, a nod to the region’s most famous dish: paella valenciana, rice cooked in a wide, shallow pan with chicken, rabbit, green beans and saffron, traditionally over a wood fire.

When

On the last Wednesday of August, around midday, at the end of a week of local festivities. Dates:

  • 2027: 25 August
  • 2028: 30 August
  • 2029: 29 August

Where

Buñol, a town about 40 km west of Valencia, Spain. Tomato fights inspired by it are now held in other countries too.

Las Fallas

Observed: 15–19 March

Celebrated at night

Bones Falles! “Happy Fallas!” in Valencian

Each March, Valencia fills its squares with hundreds of giant satirical figures, built over a year by neighbourhood groups. Days of fireworks follow, and on the night of 19 March almost all of them are set on fire.

Read more about Las Fallas

What it celebrates

The coming of spring and Saint Joseph, patron saint of carpenters, whose feast day is 19 March. One account traces the festival to Valencia’s carpenters burning the wooden boards and scraps they no longer needed at the end of winter. Over time the bonfires grew into elaborate monuments that poke fun at politicians, celebrities and everyday life. In 2016, UNESCO added the Valencia Fallas to its list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

How it’s celebrated

Each neighbourhood association (casal faller) spends the year building a falla, a monument of wood, cardboard and papier-mâché crowded with figures called ninots. They are set up in the streets by the morning of 16 March. Every day from 1 to 19 March at 2 pm, the mascletà, a deafening, rhythmic barrage of firecrackers, shakes the Plaza del Ayuntamiento.

On 17 and 18 March, falleras and falleros in traditional dress carry flowers to the Virgin of the Forsaken (Virgen de los Desamparados), and the flowers are woven into a giant mantle for her statue. On the night of 19 March, the cremà: the fallas are burned, the children’s first and the City Hall’s last, while firefighters wet the nearby buildings and keep the crowds behind barriers. One ninot each year is spared by public vote (ninot indultat) and kept in the Fallas Museum.

Food

Stalls all over the city fry bunyols (buñuelos), soft fritters often made with pumpkin, which people dip in thick hot chocolate, along with churros and roasted chestnuts. Neighbourhood associations cook huge paellas in the street for their members.

When

The main days are 15 to 19 March every year, with the daily mascletà from 1 March and the opening call (Crida) on the last Sunday of February. Main days:

  • 2027: 15–19 March
  • 2028: 15–19 March
  • 2029: 15–19 March

Where

Valencia, Spain, and many other towns of the Valencian region, which hold fallas of their own.

Sources

Falles on Wikipedia; Valencia Fallas festivity, UNESCO

Venice Carnival

Observed: February / March (the two weeks before Lent)

Celebrated by day

A Carnevale ogni scherzo vale “At Carnival, any prank goes”, an old Italian saying

In the two weeks before Lent, Venice fills with masks. People in elaborate costumes stroll the squares and bridges, as the city brings back the carnival of the old Venetian Republic.

Read more about Venice Carnival

What it celebrates

Like other carnivals, it is a last burst of feasting and fun before Lent, the forty days of fasting before Easter in the Christian calendar. Venice’s carnival goes back to the Middle Ages; legend links it to the Republic of Venice’s victory over the Patriarch of Aquileia in 1162. For centuries masks let Venetians of every class mix in the streets as equals. The festival was banned in 1797, when the Republic fell, and was revived in 1979.

How it’s celebrated

Masqueraders in silk, lace and feathers gather in Piazza San Marco and along the canals, posing for photographs without a word. Traditional masks include the bauta, which covers the whole face, the half-mask colombina, the plain white volto and the beaked plague doctor (medico della peste). The opening weekend’s Flight of the Angel (Volo dell’Angelo) sends a performer down a wire from the bell tower of San Marco to the square, and judges pick the most beautiful mask. Palaces host masked balls and dinners, and about three million visitors come each year.

Food

Frittelle (fritole in Venetian) are the Carnival sweet: fried balls of dough with raisins and pine nuts, or filled with custard or zabaglione and rolled in sugar. Galani, thin ribbons of fried pastry dusted with icing sugar, are found in every bakery.

When

It ends on Shrove Tuesday (Martedì Grasso), the day before Ash Wednesday, so it moves with Easter. It starts about two weeks before; the city publishes the programme each autumn. Last day:

  • 2027: 9 February (from about 30 January)
  • 2028: 29 February
  • 2029: 13 February

Where

Venice, Italy, mostly around Piazza San Marco. Italy’s other famous carnivals include Viareggio’s papier-mâché floats and Ivrea’s Battle of the Oranges.

Sources

Carnival of Venice on Wikipedia; Official site of the Carnevale di Venezia

Bastille Day, France’s national day

Observed: 14 July

Celebrated by day

Bonne fête nationale ! “Happy national day!”

France’s national day, the 14th of July, remembers the storming of the Bastille in 1789 and the festival of national unity a year later. Paris holds a military parade on the Champs-Élysées, and towns across the country end the day with fireworks and dances.

Read more about Bastille Day

What it celebrates

On 14 July 1789, crowds in Paris stormed the Bastille, a royal fortress and prison that stood for the power of the king, in the first days of the French Revolution. A year later, on 14 July 1790, the Fête de la Fédération on the Champ de Mars celebrated the unity of the French nation. In 1880 the Third Republic made 14 July the national holiday, recalling both events. In French it is simply the Fête nationale or le 14 Juillet; “Bastille Day” is the English name.

How it’s celebrated

In the morning, a military parade goes down the Champs-Élysées in front of the President, as it has every year since 1880 (on the Champs-Élysées since 1918), while the jets of the Patrouille de France trail blue, white and red smoke overhead. Towns and villages hold their own parades, concerts and fireworks; in Paris, a concert and fireworks at the Eiffel Tower close the day. Around the 13th and 14th, many fire stations open their courtyards for public dances, the bals des pompiers. For many people it is above all a summer day off with family and friends.

Food

There is no special dish for the 14th of July. People gather for picnics in parks and on riverbanks, family lunches and barbecues, and town squares serve drinks and snacks at the evening dances.

When

On 14 July every year, a public holiday across France.

  • 2027: Wednesday 14 July
  • 2028: Friday 14 July
  • 2029: Saturday 14 July

Where

Across France and its overseas regions. French communities abroad celebrate too, as do places with French ties such as Pondicherry in India.

Sources

Bastille Day on Wikipedia

Mardi Gras in New Orleans

Observed: February / March

Celebrated by day

Laissez les bons temps rouler ! “Let the good times roll!”, the Louisiana French motto of Carnival

New Orleans’ Carnival season builds for weeks to Mardi Gras, “Fat Tuesday”, the day before Lent. Clubs called krewes roll out parades of floats and marching bands, and riders throw beads to crowds that line the streets.

Read more about Mardi Gras

What it celebrates

Mardi Gras is French for “Fat Tuesday”, the last day of feasting before Lent in the Christian calendar. French settlers brought the custom to Louisiana: in 1699 the explorers Iberville and Bienville named a spot on the Mississippi Point du Mardi Gras. Today it is a celebration of New Orleans itself, its music, food and neighbourhoods, for people of every faith, and a legal holiday in the city.

How it’s celebrated

The season opens on Twelfth Night, 6 January, and the parades fill its last two weeks. Krewes, private clubs, build themed floats and ride them in masks, throwing beads, doubloons and cups to people shouting “Throw me something, mister!”. Night parades are lit by flambeau carriers, a role first held by enslaved and free Black men.

On Mardi Gras morning the Zulu Social Aid and Pleasure Club, founded by Black New Orleanians, parades with its prized hand-painted coconuts, followed by Rex, the “King of Carnival”, whose colours of purple, green and gold (justice, faith and power) have marked the season since 1872. In Black neighbourhoods, the Mardi Gras Indians, also called Black Masking Indians, come out in beaded and feathered suits they spend all year sewing by hand. About 1.4 million visitors come.

Food

King cake, a ring of sweet dough iced in purple, green and gold, is eaten from Twelfth Night to Mardi Gras. A tiny plastic baby is hidden inside, and whoever finds it brings the next cake. Parade crowds eat po’boys, fried chicken, gumbo and jambalaya.

When

Mardi Gras falls 47 days before Easter, on a Tuesday between 3 February and 9 March; the biggest parades run in the twelve days before it. Mardi Gras Day:

  • 2027: 9 February
  • 2028: 29 February
  • 2029: 13 February

Where

New Orleans, Louisiana, USA, and along the Gulf Coast; Mobile, Alabama, says its celebration is the oldest in the country. In rural Cajun Louisiana, riders on horseback go farm to farm in the Courir de Mardi Gras.

Sources

Mardi Gras in New Orleans on Wikipedia; King cake on Wikipedia

Coachella Valley Music and Arts Festival

Observed: Two weekends in April

Celebrated at night

Coachella is a modern music festival rather than a tradition. Since 1999 it has filled a polo club in the California desert, now for two April weekends, with big-name concerts, giant art installations and about 250,000 visitors.

Read more about Coachella

What it celebrates

Music and art, not a religious or historical event. Coachella is a commercial festival run by the concert promoter Goldenvoice, part of AEG, and first held over two days in October 1999. It became one of the biggest and highest-earning music festivals in the world, and its line-ups, shows and fashion are followed online far beyond California. It belongs on a festival atlas as a newer kind of festival: a ticketed event that many people plan their year around.

How it’s celebrated

Each weekend runs from Friday to Sunday with the same line-up. Stages and tents named after deserts, such as the Sahara tent for electronic music, run from midday until after midnight. Huge art installations, many lit up at night, stand on the lawns beside a Ferris wheel, and many visitors camp on site. Since 2011 sets have been streamed live online. Tickets are expensive, starting at about 550 US dollars for 2027, and the festival has drawn criticism over its cost and over visitors wearing Native American–style headdresses.

Food

Food comes from restaurant and street-food stalls across the grounds. The Coachella Valley around it grows most of the dates in the United States, and a date shake, a milkshake blended with dates, is the local speciality.

When

Two weekends in April, usually the second and third. Dates are announced about a year ahead:

  • 2027: 9–11 and 16–18 April
  • 2028: not yet announced
  • 2029: not yet announced

Where

The Empire Polo Club in Indio, California, USA. The same grounds host Stagecoach, a country-music festival, the following weekend.

Sources

Viña del Mar International Song Festival

Observed: Six nights in late February

Celebrated at night

¡El Monstruo! “The Monster”, the nickname of the festival’s famously demanding crowd

Latin America’s oldest and best-known song festival: since 1960, six summer nights in Viña del Mar’s open-air amphitheatre, broadcast live, where a famously demanding crowd decides who goes home with a silver seagull.

Read more about Viña del Mar Festival

What it celebrates

Popular music, from Chilean folk to Latin pop, rock, reggaeton and comedy. The festival is an event, not a tradition with religious or historical roots: the city of Viña del Mar founded it in 1960 as a song contest to bring summer visitors to its seaside. It grew into one of the biggest music shows in the Spanish-speaking world, and a good night there can launch a career across Latin America.

How it’s celebrated

Each night, about 15,000 people fill the amphitheatre of the Quinta Vergara park for concerts by international stars and Chilean acts, stand-up comedians and an international and a folk song contest. The audience, nicknamed el Monstruo (“the Monster”), is famous for its verdicts: it chants for performers it loves to receive the Gaviota de Plata (silver seagull) or Gaviota de Oro (gold), and boos those it does not, sometimes off the stage. The show is broadcast live on television and online to an audience across the Americas and beyond, and the week opens with a red-carpet gala.

Food

It is the height of Chile’s summer, so the classic treat is mote con huesillo, a cold drink of dried peaches in syrup with husked wheat, sold at stalls on the coast, along with empanadas de pino, baked pastries filled with beef, onion, egg and olive.

When

Six nights in the second half of February, with the dates set by the organisers each year:

Guelaguetza

Observed: The two Mondays after 16 July

Celebrated by day

¡Viva Oaxaca! “Long live Oaxaca!”

Each July, the city of Oaxaca hosts the Guelaguetza. Delegations from all over the state, many of them Indigenous communities, share their dances, music and dress on the hill of El Fortín, and dancers end by sharing gifts from their region with the audience.

Read more about Guelaguetza

What it celebrates

The many cultures of the state of Oaxaca, one of Mexico’s most Indigenous regions, home to Zapotec, Mixtec, Mazatec, Chinantec, Mixe and other peoples. The word comes from Zapotec and names a custom of reciprocity: giving gifts and help, such as corn or work for a wedding, that will be returned one day.

The festival is tied to the feast of the Virgin of Carmen on 16 July and is often linked to older maize celebrations for the goddess Centéotl, though there is no firm evidence of a pre-Hispanic origin. Its modern form dates from 1932, when the city marked its 400th anniversary.

How it’s celebrated

On the Saturday before, delegations from Oaxaca’s eight regions parade through the city with their bands. On each of the two Mondays, called the Lunes del Cerro (“Mondays on the Hill”), they perform in the open-air Guelaguetza auditorium on the Cerro del Fortín, in the morning and again in the afternoon. Each region brings its own dances: in Flor de Piña, from Tuxtepec, women dance with pineapples on their shoulders, and the Danza de la Pluma, danced by men in tall feathered headdresses, closes the festival.

A young woman chosen to represent Centéotl, for her knowledge of her community’s traditions, presides. Seats in the front sections are ticketed, and some communities have criticised the festival for turning traditions into a show for tourists; in 2006, protesters held a free “People’s Guelaguetza” of their own.

Food

Oaxaca is one of Mexico’s great food regions: mole negro, a dark sauce of chiles, chocolate and spices; tlayudas, large crisp tortillas with beans, quesillo cheese and meat; tamales; chocolate and tejate, a cold drink of maize and cacao; and mezcal, with a mezcal fair held in the city at the same time.

When

On the two Mondays after 16 July. When the first would fall on 18 July, the anniversary of Benito Juárez’s death, both move a week later. Dates:

  • 2027: 19 and 26 July
  • 2028: 17 and 24 July
  • 2029: 23 and 30 July

Where

Oaxaca de Juárez, the capital of the state of Oaxaca in southern Mexico. Oaxacan communities in the United States, such as in Los Angeles, hold Guelaguetzas of their own.

Observed: 2 February

Celebrated by day

Odoyá! the greeting to Iemanjá, often translated as “mother of the waters”

On 2 February, crowds dressed in white and blue gather on the beach of Rio Vermelho in Salvador to honour Iemanjá, the Afro-Brazilian orixá of the sea, with flowers and gifts that fishing boats carry out to the ocean.

Read more about Festa de Iemanjá

What it celebrates

Iemanjá (Yemọja in Yoruba) is an orixá, a divinity of Candomblé, the Afro-Brazilian religion rooted in the faith of Yoruba people brought to Brazil in slavery; she is also honoured in Umbanda. She is the mother of the waters and queen of the sea, protector of fishermen, mothers and families.

In Salvador the festival grew from the offerings of the Rio Vermelho fishermen, who began taking their gifts to the sea there in 1924. Many Catholics and people of no particular faith join in too; 2 February is also the Catholic feast of Our Lady of Candles (Nossa Senhora das Candeias).

How it’s celebrated

From before dawn, people queue at the fishermen’s house on the beach to leave offerings for Iemanjá: flowers, perfume and handwritten wishes, gathered in large woven baskets. Candomblé priestesses and their communities (terreiros) sing and dance to drums, and many people dress in white and blue, her colours. In the late afternoon, about 300 boats led by the fishermen carry the baskets and a main gift, prepared by a terreiro, out to sea and give them to the water.

A campaign asks for biodegradable gifts, such as flowers and fruit, instead of plastic and bottles. Then Rio Vermelho’s streets turn into a party. For many it is a religious day, so visitors are asked not to interrupt ceremonies.

Bahia’s cooking carries strong African roots. Acarajé, black-eyed-pea fritters fried in dendê palm oil and filled with spicy shrimp and vatapá, is sold by baianas de acarajé in white dress; it is also a sacred food in Candomblé, and the baianas’ craft is protected as part of Brazil’s heritage. Moqueca baiana, a fish stew with coconut milk and dendê oil, is another favourite.

When

On 2 February every year.

  • 2028: Wednesday 2 February
  • 2029: Friday 2 February

Where

Rio Vermelho beach in Salvador, Bahia, Brazil. In Uruguay, crowds gather on the beaches of Montevideo on 2 February to leave gifts for Iemanjá.

Gnaoua World Music Festival

Observed: Three days in late June

Celebrated at night

Every June the Atlantic port of Essaouira fills with the sound of the guembri, as masters of the Gnawa tradition share open-air stages with jazz, rock and world-music guests. Founded in 1998, it is a public music festival that carried a Moroccan spiritual music to a worldwide audience.

Read more about Gnaoua World Music Festival

What it celebrates

Gnawa (also spelled Gnaoua) is a Moroccan tradition of music and spiritual practice. Its roots lie with people brought to Morocco from sub-Saharan Africa, many of them enslaved, and it blends African, Arab-Muslim and Berber elements. Gnawa masters, called maâlems, lead brotherhoods whose all-night healing ceremony, the lila (“night”), calls on ancestors and spirits with sacred songs. UNESCO added Gnawa to its list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity in 2019.

The festival is something different from the lila. It is a public music festival, founded in 1998 in Essaouira by the Moroccan producer Neila Tazi and run by the events company A3. It puts Gnawa masters on stage with invited musicians from jazz, rock, pop and other traditions, and many of its concerts are free. The lila remains a religious and healing ritual, held in homes and in the community’s own spaces, not a show; visitors who are invited to one should treat it with respect.

How it’s celebrated

For three days in late June, concerts run into the night on stages around Essaouira. A Gnawa group is led by a maâlem playing the guembri, a three-stringed bass lute, while the others answer in song and clap the qraqeb, iron castanets, as they dance. Many sets pair a maâlem with guests from far away: in 2026 they came from port cities from Lebanon to Cameroon and Brazil. Press reports speak of several hundred thousand visitors over the days.

Food

Essaouira is a fishing port, and its signature meal is sardines or other fresh fish grilled on charcoal at the harbour stalls. Harira, Morocco’s tomato, lentil and chickpea soup, is eaten across the country, as are tagines and mint tea.

When

Three days in late June, in recent years Thursday to Saturday. The dates are announced about a year ahead:

  • 2027: Thursday 24 – Saturday 26 June
  • 2028: not yet announced
  • 2029: not yet announced

Where

Essaouira, on Morocco’s Atlantic coast about 175 km west of Marrakech. Its walled old town and harbour are a UNESCO World Heritage Site; the main stage is at Place Moulay Hassan, between the old town and the harbour.

What it celebrates

Freedom, music and community. At the Cape, enslaved people were traditionally given the day after New Year’s Day off, and they spent it celebrating in their own way. Slavery at the Cape was abolished in 1834, and the gatherings continued as part of the story of Cape Town’s Cape Malay and coloured communities, many of them descended from enslaved people brought from Africa, India and Southeast Asia. Troupes took up costumes and parading in the 19th century. Today it is a celebration of that heritage and of the neighbourhoods the troupes come from.

Names matter here. The Afrikaans Kaapse Klopse (“Cape troupes”) and Tweede Nuwe Jaar are what many in the community say; the official English name is the Cape Town Minstrel Carnival. How it’s celebrated

Troupes of up to a thousand members, each with its own colours, brass band and drum major, march through the city and are judged on categories such as best dress, music and drum major. Participants wear matching satin or sequinned suits, hats and bright umbrellas, play trumpets, banjos and the ghoema, the barrel drum whose beat runs through the carnival, and sing in Afrikaans and English. For 2026 organisers asked for a new route through Green Point into the stadium, so the route and even the day can change from year to year.

Food

The Cape Malay kitchen is the carnival’s flavour: koesisters, spiced, syrup-dipped doughnuts rolled in coconut; samoosas, small fried pastries; and bobotie, a baked, curry-spiced mince dish under an egg custard.

Cape Town, South Africa. The parade has long run through the city centre and past the Bo-Kaap, the historic Cape Malay neighbourhood.

Nowruz

Observed: 20 or 21 March (the spring equinox)

Celebrated by day

نوروز مبارک Nowruz mobārak, “Blessed Nowruz”

Nowruz, “new day”, is the Persian New Year, marked at the spring equinox. For some 3,000 years families in Iran and many other countries have cleaned their homes, set a table of symbolic foods and spent days visiting, feasting and gathering outdoors.

Read more about Nowruz

What it celebrates

The arrival of spring. Nowruz (Persian for “new day”) begins at the March equinox, the first day of the Iranian calendar, and has its roots in ancient Persian and Zoroastrian tradition. For most it is a secular family festival, though a holy day for Zoroastrians, Baháʼís and some Ismaili Muslims.

It is not any one country’s festival: peoples from the Balkans to Central and South Asia celebrate it, under names such as Navruz, Newroz and Nauryz, and the United Nations marks 21 March as the International Day of Nowruz.

Kurdish communities in Iran, Iraq, Turkey and Syria celebrate Newroz too, often with large bonfires, and in some places it has also been a day for expressing Kurdish identity. Governments have at times restricted it: Kurdish Newroz gatherings in Turkey and Syria have faced bans, restrictions and mass arrests, and in 2022 Afghanistan’s Taliban government cancelled Nowruz as a public holiday, while saying people could still celebrate privately.

How it’s celebrated

Weeks ahead, homes get a spring clean, khaneh-tekani (“shaking of the house”). The haft-sin table holds seven items whose Persian names begin with the letter sin, such as sabzeh (sprouts) for rebirth and sib (apple) for beauty.

On the last Wednesday evening of the year, Chaharshanbe Suri, people jump over small bonfires. At the equinox families gather at the table and exchange gifts, often money (eidi), then spend days visiting relatives. On the thirteenth day, Sizdah Bedar, everyone picnics outdoors.

Food

On Nowruz eve Iranian families traditionally eat sabzi polo ba mahi, herbed rice with fish; kuku sabzi, a herb frittata, is a staple. Across Central Asia and Afghanistan, the sprouted-wheat pudding sumalak is slow-cooked overnight.

When

The moment of the March equinox. In Iran the year starts on the equinox day if it comes before noon in Tehran, otherwise the next. Other countries may mark 20 or 21 March:

  • 2027: Sunday 21 March (the equinox is just before midnight on 20 March in Tehran)
  • 2028: Monday 20 March
  • 2029: Tuesday 20 March

Where

Iran, where it is the biggest holiday, and widely across Afghanistan, Azerbaijan, Iraq, Central Asia, Turkey, Pakistan, India and the diaspora. The pin sits in Iran; the other countries on UNESCO’s list light up with it.

What it celebrates

Winter, and a city that makes the most of it. Sapporo, the capital of Hokkaido, Japan’s northernmost main island, gets several metres of snow each winter. How it’s celebrated

The festival has three sites. Awa Odori

Observed: 12–15 August

Celebrated at night

踊る阿呆に見る阿呆 Odoru ahō ni miru ahō, “Fools who dance and fools who watch”, from the festival song

For four nights in mid-August, during Obon, the streets of Tokushima fill with groups of dancers and musicians moving to the two-beat rhythm of shamisen, drums, flutes and bells. It is Japan’s best-known bon odori, the dancing of the season when the spirits of ancestors are said to come home.

Read more about Awa Odori

What it celebrates

Awa Odori means “the Awa dance”; Awa is the old name of what is now Tokushima Prefecture, on the island of Shikoku. It grew out of bon odori, the dances of Obon, the Buddhist season in mid-August when families welcome back the spirits of their ancestors. Today it is above all a joyful town festival, and many people come home for Obon to dance it.

A popular story says it began in the 1580s, when the lord Hachisuka Iemasa celebrated the completion of Tokushima Castle, but the story first appears in 1908. What is certain is that Tokushima’s Obon dancing was already large and boisterous in the 17th century, when the domain limited it to three days. It faded in the late 19th century and was revived in the 1920s, when the name “Awa Odori” was coined.

How it’s celebrated

Dancers and musicians perform in groups called ren, from neighbourhood clubs and companies to famous groups with long histories. Each evening the ren parade through the streets and across staged dancing areas (enbujō), to shamisen, taiko drums, shinobue flutes and a small hand bell, the kane, with calls such as “Yattosa, yattosa!”

Women dance upright in summer kimono and folded straw amigasa hats, with small, graceful hand movements; men dance low, knees bent and arms raised, a style many women and children now dance too. The festival song’s best-known line says that dancers and watchers are fools alike, so you may as well dance, and open groups called niwaka ren let visitors join in.

Food

Tokushima has its own ramen: a dark broth of pork bones and soy sauce, topped with sweet-simmered pork belly and often a raw egg. The prefecture also grows most of Japan’s sudachi, a small green citrus squeezed over grilled fish and noodles.

When

Every year on 12 to 15 August, during Obon; in recent years an opening show has been held indoors on 11 August:

  • 2027: 12–15 August
  • 2028: 12–15 August
  • 2029: 12–15 August

Where

Tokushima city, on Shikoku, with dancing areas around the city centre at the foot of Mount Bizan. Tokyo’s Kōenji district holds a large Awa Odori each August.

Observed: The 15th day of the first lunar month (February or early March)

Celebrated at night

元宵节快乐 Yuánxiāo jié kuàilè, “Happy Lantern Festival”

On the year’s first full moon, fifteen days after Chinese New Year, the New Year season ends in light. Streets and parks glow with lantern displays, people try to solve riddles hung from the lanterns, and families share sweet rice balls, round like the moon, as a sign of being together.

Read more about Lantern Festival

What it celebrates

The Lantern Festival (元宵节, Yuánxiāo jié, “festival of the first night”) falls on the 15th day of the first lunar month, the first full moon of the year, and closes the Chinese New Year season. Where New Year’s Eve is spent at home, the Lantern Festival is about going out: in imperial China it was one of the few nights when the curfew was lifted and everyone, young women included, could walk the streets, which is why it is sometimes called a Chinese Valentine’s Day.

Its beginnings are told in several ways, from a Han dynasty emperor ordering lamps lit in honour of the Buddha to the birthday of the Daoist Heavenly Official (Tiānguān). Lantern displays were already lavish under the Tang dynasty.

How it’s celebrated

Lanterns hang in streets, temples and parks, from simple red paper globes to huge displays of dragons, zodiac animals and scenes from legend. Lantern fairs such as the Qinhuai Lantern Fair in Nanjing and the lantern show in Zigong draw crowds, and riddles hung from the lanterns (cāi dēngmí, “guessing lantern riddles”) may win a small prize. Lion and dragon dances and fireworks fill the evening. In Malaysia and Singapore, where the night is called Chap Goh Mei, some young women toss mandarin oranges into the water in the hope of finding a partner.

Food

The festival’s food is the glutinous rice ball, round like the full moon and a symbol of a family that is whole. In northern China they are called yuánxiāo and are rolled in dry rice flour; in the south they are tāngyuán, wrapped in a soft dough. Both are usually filled with black sesame, red bean or peanut paste and served in a sweet soup.

  • 2029: 27 February

Across China, Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan, and in Chinese communities around the world. Korea marks the same full moon as Daeboreum. The pin sits in Nanjing, home of the Qinhuai Lantern Fair.

Loy Krathong

Observed: The full moon of the twelfth Thai lunar month (usually November)

Celebrated at night

สุขสันต์วันลอยกระทง Suk san wan Loi Krathong, “Happy Loy Krathong”

On the full-moon night of the twelfth Thai lunar month, people across Thailand float small candlelit baskets of banana leaf and flowers on rivers, canals and ponds, to thank the water, let go of the year’s misfortunes and make a wish. In Chiang Mai it meets Yi Peng, the northern festival of lanterns.

Read more about Loy Krathong

What it celebrates

Loi means “to float”, and a krathong is a small floating vessel. The festival thanks the waters that give life and asks their forgiveness for using and polluting them; many Thais honour Phra Mae Khongkha, the goddess of water. For many it is also a Buddhist act: in 1863 King Mongkut wrote that the candle honours the Buddha and that floating it away lets go of anger and wrongdoing.

A popular story says the festival began at the 13th-century court of Sukhothai, with a court lady called Nang Noppamas who made the first krathong. Historians regard that story as a 19th-century literary invention; the festival as most Thais know it today took shape in the 20th century.

How it’s celebrated

Krathong are traditionally made from a slice of banana trunk wrapped in folded banana leaves and decorated with flowers, with a candle and three incense sticks. After dark, people light them, make a wish and set them on the water. Temples and riversides hold fairs and concerts, and Sukhothai Historical Park stages light-and-sound shows among its ruins.

In the north, Loy Krathong falls together with Yi Peng, the Lanna festival of lights, when homes and temples are hung with lanterns. Sky lanterns (khom loi), which endanger aircraft and start fires, are now banned or allowed only at approved events and places. Foam krathong are discouraged or banned too, and many are now made of bread or other materials that break down.

Food

Loy Krathong has no single festival dish. Its fairs sell Thai street food, such as khanom krok, small coconut-rice pancakes cooked in a dimpled iron pan, and in Chiang Mai northern dishes such as khao soi, a curry noodle soup.

When

On the evening of the full moon of the twelfth month of the Thai lunar calendar, usually in November. In Chiang Mai the celebrations last about three days:

  • 2027: 13 November
  • 2028: 1 November
  • 2029: 20 November

Where

Across Thailand, wherever there is water; Sukhothai, Chiang Mai and the Chao Phraya River in Bangkok are best known. Neighbouring countries mark the same full moon with festivals of their own, such as Cambodia’s Bon Om Touk and Myanmar’s Tazaungdaing.

For three days at the full moon that ends the rainy season, as the Tonle Sap River turns back toward the Mekong, Cambodia celebrates its Water Festival with long-boat races, illuminated floats and a midnight salute to the moon. In a normal year, millions travel to Phnom Penh for the races in front of the Royal Palace.

Read more about Bon Om Touk

What it celebrates

Bon Om Touk (បុណ្យអុំទូក), “festival of rowing boats”, is Cambodia’s Water Festival. It marks the end of the rainy season, when the Tonle Sap River, which the swollen Mekong pushed upstream into the Tonle Sap lake during the monsoon, reverses and flows back out. It gives thanks to the river for water, fish and fertile soil, and to the moon.

Boat racing is thought to go back to the Angkor era: carvings at the Bayon temple, built in the late 12th century, show war canoes. The first festival on record, under King Norodom, was held in 1873.

The festival is also remembered for a tragedy: on its last night in 2010, a crowd crush on the bridge to Koh Pich (Diamond Island) in Phnom Penh killed 347 people. A memorial stupa now stands near the site.

How it’s celebrated

Over three days, hundreds of long wooden boats, many with a dragon or naga head and up to about 80 rowers, race in pairs along the river in front of the Royal Palace; in 2024, 348 boats took part. After dark, illuminated floats (bandet bratib) sail past with fireworks, and on the night of the full moon families salute the moon with offerings (sampeah preah khae).

Some years the Phnom Penh celebrations are called off, after floods, deaths in the royal family, elections or COVID-19. In 2025 and 2026 the government cancelled them because of the border conflict with Thailand, while keeping the public holiday and leaving provinces free to hold their own races.

Food

Ak ambok (អកអំបុក) is the festival’s own food: newly harvested rice, toasted, pounded flat and mixed with grated coconut, banana and palm sugar. Families eat it at midnight during the moon salute.

When

Three days around the full moon of the Khmer month of Kadeuk, usually in November and sometimes late October. The government confirms the dates each year; the dates below are expected, not yet announced:

  • 2027: about 12–14 November
  • 2028: about 31 October – 2 November
  • 2029: about 19–21 November

Where

Phnom Penh, on the riverfront where the Tonle Sap meets the Mekong, and provinces across Cambodia, such as Siem Reap, which hold their own races.

Tết

Observed: The first day of the lunar year (late January or February)

Celebrated by day

Chúc mừng năm mới! “Happy New Year” in Vietnamese

Tết Nguyên Đán, the Vietnamese Lunar New Year, is the biggest holiday of the Vietnamese year. Families travel home, wrap square bánh chưng rice cakes, honour their ancestors and fill the house with peach blossom or yellow mai, and the first visitor through the door is said to set the luck for the year.

Read more about Tết

What it celebrates

Tết Nguyên Đán means “festival of the first morning”. It welcomes the new year of the lunisolar calendar and the start of spring, a time to honour ancestors, settle debts and quarrels and begin afresh. Tết usually falls on the same day as Chinese New Year, but it is a festival in its own right, with its own customs, foods and beliefs. Vietnam sets its calendar by Hanoi time, so the date occasionally differs, and its zodiac has the cat in place of the rabbit.

How it’s celebrated

Tết begins a week early, when families send the Kitchen Gods (Ông Công, Ông Táo) to heaven to report on the household, often releasing live carp to carry them. Homes are cleaned and decorated with pink peach blossom (hoa đào) in the north and yellow mai in the south, and a five-fruit tray (mâm ngũ quả) goes on the ancestors’ altar.

At giao thừa, the moment the year turns, families make offerings to their ancestors and cities hold firework displays. The first person to enter a home in the new year (xông đất) is thought to bring its luck. In the first days people visit relatives, teachers and friends, give children red envelopes of lucky money (lì xì) and visit pagodas and family graves.

Food

Every family table has bánh chưng, a square cake of sticky rice, mung beans and pork wrapped in dong leaves and boiled for hours; in the south the same filling goes into the cylindrical bánh tét. Alongside come giò lụa, a smooth pork roll, pickled onions and, in the south, thịt kho, pork and eggs braised in coconut water.

When

On the first day of the first lunar month, usually the same day as Chinese New Year, between late January and 20 February. The holiday lasts about a week:

  • 2027: 6 February
  • 2028: 26 January
  • 2029: 13 February

Where

Across Vietnam, and in Vietnamese communities around the world. The pin sits in the middle of Vietnam, since Tết belongs to the whole country.

Thimphu Tshechu

Observed: The 10th day of the 8th Bhutanese month (September or October)

Celebrated by day

Thimphu Tshechu is one of Bhutan’s largest religious festivals, held for three days beside Tashichho Dzong, the fortress that houses the government and the central monk body. Monks and laymen in masks and silk costumes perform cham, sacred dances that tell the stories of Guru Rinpoche, and families in their finest gho and kira come to watch, picnic and receive blessings.

Read more about Thimphu Tshechu

What it celebrates

A tshechu (“tenth day”) honours Guru Rinpoche (Padmasambhava), the eighth-century teacher who brought tantric Buddhism to the Himalayas; his deeds are said to have fallen on the tenth day of the lunar month. Watching the dances is not entertainment alone: for Bhutanese Buddhists it is a way of gaining merit and blessings, and the dances teach the Buddhist path, the defeat of evil and what awaits the soul after death. Thimphu’s tshechu was introduced in 1670, under the 4th Desi, Tenzin Rabgye.

How it’s celebrated

Each day brings a fixed order of cham. In the Dance of the Black Hats, monks with drums claim the ground from evil; in the Dance of the Four Stags, Guru Rinpoche rides the stag that was once the King of the Wind. Masked jesters called atsara move through the crowd between dances, teasing onlookers and keeping order, and groups of women sing and dance folk songs between the masked plays.

Families arrive early for a good spot, dressed in their best gho and kira, and stay all day, sharing food and greeting relatives they rarely see. At many tshechus, a giant appliqué thongdrel of Guru Rinpoche is unrolled before dawn on the final morning, and simply seeing it is believed to cleanse the viewer of sin. Visitors are welcome, but are expected to dress modestly and to keep out of the dancers’ way.

Food

A tshechu is also a family picnic. Baskets hold red rice and ema datshi, Bhutan’s national dish of chillies cooked in cheese, along with kewa datshi (with potato), dried meat and suja, salted butter tea, and the homemade spirit ara is often shared.

When

On the 10th day of the 8th month of the Bhutanese lunar calendar, with the festival running from the 9th to the 11th, so the dates move between mid-September and mid-October:

  • 2027: 10–12 October (tentative)
  • 2028: not yet announced
  • 2029: not yet announced

Where

At Tashichho Dzong in Thimphu, Bhutan’s capital. Every district holds its own tshechu, and Paro’s and Bumthang’s are also well known. This atlas puts the pin at Thimphu.

Tihar

Observed: Five days in Kartik (October or November)

Celebrated at night

तिहारको शुभकामना “Best wishes for Tihar” in Nepali

Tihar is Nepal’s five-day festival of lights and the country’s second-biggest holiday after Dashain. Each day honours someone different: crows, then dogs, then cows and Lakshmi, the goddess of wealth, then oxen, and finally brothers, who receive a seven-coloured tika from their sisters. Homes glow with oil lamps and marigold garlands, and children go from door to door singing deusi and bhailo songs.

Read more about Tihar

What it celebrates

Tihar falls at the same time as Diwali in India and shares its night of Lakshmi Puja, but its order of days is Nepal’s own. It honours the animals that live alongside people, and the bond between brothers and sisters, linked to the story of Yamuna, who kept her brother Yama, the god of death, from his duties until her rituals were done. Hindus across Nepal celebrate it, and so do Newar Buddhists, who call it Swanti and begin the new year of their own calendar, Nepal Sambat, on the fourth day.

How it’s celebrated

On Kaag Tihar, families leave rice and sweets on rooftops for crows, the messengers of Yama. On Kukur Tihar, dogs, pets and strays alike, are garlanded with marigolds, given a red tika and fed a good meal. Gai Tihar honours the cow; that night, on Lakshmi Puja, homes are cleaned, doorways painted with rangoli and oil lamps (diyo) and lights lit to welcome Lakshmi. On the fourth day oxen are honoured (Goru Puja), and Newars perform Mha Puja, a worship of one’s own body and soul.

The festival ends with Bhai Tika. Sisters circle their brothers, pour a line of oil around them, place a seven-coloured tika on their foreheads and a garland of makhamali flowers around their necks, and pray for a long life. Brothers return the tika and give gifts. In the evenings, groups go door to door singing bhailo and deusi songs for money, fruit and sel roti.

Food

The festival treat is sel roti, a sweet ring of rice-flour batter fried until crisp outside and soft inside. Plates for Bhai Tika hold fruit, nuts (walnuts are traditional) and sweets such as anarasa.

When

From the 13th day of the waning moon of Kartik to the 2nd day of the waxing moon, so in October or November. Exact days are set each year by Nepal’s calendar committee:

  • 2026: 7–11 November
  • 2027: Lakshmi Puja on 29 October
  • 2028: not yet announced
  • 2029: not yet announced

Where

Across Nepal, and in Darjeeling, Sikkim and Nepali-speaking communities in India, Bhutan, Myanmar and around the world. This atlas puts the pin in the middle of Nepal.

Durga Puja

Observed: Five days in Ashvin (September or October)

Celebrated at night

শুভ বিজয়া Shubho Bijoya, “Happy Bijoya”, the Bengali greeting from the last day on

Durga Puja celebrates the goddess Durga’s victory over the buffalo demon Mahishasura. For five days, cities in eastern India and Bangladesh fill with pandals, temporary temples of bamboo and cloth that hold clay images of Durga, and crowds move from one to the next late into the night. Durga Puja in Kolkata is on UNESCO’s list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Read more about Durga Puja

What it celebrates

In the Hindu story, the demon Mahishasura could not be killed by any man or god, so the gods combined their powers into the goddess Durga, who fought him for days and killed him on the tenth. The festival marks that victory of good over evil, and is also seen as Durga’s yearly visit to her parents’ home with her children, Lakshmi, Saraswati, Ganesha and Kartikeya.

It begins a week before the main days, on Mahalaya, when people make offerings to their ancestors and listen to Mahishasuramardini, a dawn radio programme of hymns aired in Bengal since the 1930s.

How it’s celebrated

Months before, artisans in districts such as Kumartuli in Kolkata shape the images from straw, bamboo and river clay. Neighbourhood committees build pandals, from traditional temples to elaborate art installations. From Shashthi, the sixth day, the images are unveiled and families dress in new clothes to go “pandal hopping” through the night. On the main days drummers play the dhak and people dance the dhunuchi naach with clay pots of burning incense.

On Bijoya Dashami, the last day, married women smear vermilion on the goddess and on each other in sindoor khela. Then the images are carried to rivers and lakes and immersed, as Durga returns to her husband, Shiva. Afterwards, people visit relatives, touch the feet of their elders and share sweets with the greeting “Shubho Bijoya”. Because paint and plastic from the images pollute the water, many cities now limit immersion to set places.

Food

At the pandals, the goddess’s bhog is offered and then shared with everyone: khichuri of rice and lentils, labra (mixed vegetables), fritters, chutney and payesh, a rice pudding. Stalls sell rolls and phuchka, and sweets such as roshogolla are passed round on Bijoya Dashami.

When

In the bright half of the month of Ashvin, between late September and late October. The main days run from Shashthi to Dashami:

  • 2026: 17–20 October (Saptami to Dashami)
  • 2027: 6–10 October
  • 2028: 25–28 September
  • 2029: not yet listed

Where

Above all in West Bengal, especially Kolkata, and in Bangladesh, Tripura and Assam, and by Bengali communities around the world. The pin sits in Kolkata, and Bangladesh is lit faintly.

Vivid Sydney

Observed: About three weeks from late May to June

Celebrated at night

Vivid Sydney is a festival of light, music and ideas that turns Sydney Harbour into an outdoor gallery for three weeks each winter. Artists project moving images onto the sails of the Opera House and light up buildings, parks and the harbour, while concerts, talks and food events fill the city. Started in 2009 as a small light walk, it now draws millions of visitors each year.

Read more about Vivid Sydney

What it celebrates

Vivid is a modern event, not a traditional festival. It began in 2009 as “Smart Light Sydney”, an idea for a festival of low-energy lighting art, and is run by Destination NSW, the state’s tourism agency, to bring visitors to the city in the quiet winter months. It celebrates light art, music and creative ideas, and it works on Gadigal land: the Opera House projections often feature First Nations artists, as in 2022, when paintings by Martumili artists covered the sails.

How it’s celebrated

After dark, visitors walk a light trail around Circular Quay, The Rocks and Barangaroo, past dozens of installations, many of them interactive. The highlight is “Lighting of the Sails”, an animated work projected across the Opera House each night, and there are light displays on the Harbour Bridge and drone shows over the water. Vivid Music brings concerts to the Opera House and other venues, and Vivid Ideas brings talks and workshops by designers and thinkers. Most of the light walk is free.

Food

Vivid Food, added in 2023, brings food events, pop-up dinners and night markets to the festival, with food trucks along the light walk. There is no traditional dish: the food is Sydney’s own mix of cuisines.

When

For about three weeks each year, from late May into June, when Sydney’s nights are long:

  • 2027: 28 May – 19 June
  • 2028: not yet announced
  • 2029: not yet announced

Where

Around Sydney Harbour, from the Opera House and Circular Quay to The Rocks, Barangaroo and Darling Harbour, with programmes in other parts of the city.

Melbourne Cup

Observed: The first Tuesday in November

Celebrated by day

The Melbourne Cup is a 3,200-metre horse race run at Flemington Racecourse since 1861, and its nickname, “the race that stops a nation”, is close to the truth: at 3 pm on the first Tuesday in November, offices, pubs and schools across Australia pause to watch. It is a sporting event more than a festival, but for many Australians it is a day of dressing up, picnics and a small bet.

Read more about Melbourne Cup

What it celebrates

Nothing religious or historic: it celebrates the race itself and the social day around it. The Cup was first run in 1861, won by a horse called Archer, and has been run every year since. It is the high point of the Melbourne Cup Carnival, a week of racing at Flemington, and Cup Day is a public holiday in Melbourne and much of Victoria. Phar Lap, who won in 1930, is still the most famous winner; Makybe Diva is the only horse to have won three times (2003–2005).

How it’s celebrated

At Flemington, crowds of up to 100,000 dress up, in suits and hats or fascinators. Fashions on the Field, a style competition started in 1962, is part of the day, and the lawns fill with picnics and marquees. Across the country, offices run “sweeps”, drawing horses’ names from a hat, and many people place the only bet of their year.

The day has critics too. Seven horses died from injuries in the race between 2013 and 2020, which led to reviews and stricter veterinary checks, and there are concerns about gambling and reported spikes in domestic violence on Cup Day. Some Australians boycott the race for these reasons.

Food

A Cup Day lunch or picnic is the custom: at the track, on the lawns or at a barbecue at home, often with champagne and a sweep. At work, many offices hold a lunch timed so everyone can watch the race at 3 pm.

When

On the first Tuesday in November, at 3 pm Melbourne time:

  • 2026: 3 November
  • 2027: 2 November
  • 2028: 7 November
  • 2029: 6 November

Where

At Flemington Racecourse in Melbourne, on the Maribyrnong River, and watched across Australia and New Zealand.

Adelaide Fringe

Observed: About a month from mid-February to March

Celebrated at night

The Adelaide Fringe is the world’s second-biggest annual arts festival, after Edinburgh’s, and the biggest in the Southern Hemisphere. For a month each summer, more than 1,300 shows, from comedy, cabaret and circus to theatre and music, fill about 500 venues across the city, and anyone can take part: no one selects the programme.

Read more about Adelaide Fringe

What it celebrates

The Fringe celebrates open access to the arts. It began in 1960, when local artists left out of the first Adelaide Festival of Arts staged their own shows alongside it, and it became its own organisation in 1975. It has been held every year since 2007. Because it is open-access, any artist who registers and finds a venue can appear, from first-timers to famous names, which is why the programme is so large and so mixed.

How it’s celebrated

The heart of the Fringe is in the city’s parklands. The Garden of Unearthly Delights, in Rundle Park (Kadlitpina), fills with spiegeltents, rides and bars, and Gluttony, in Rymill Park (Murlawirrapurka), with dozens of pop-up venues; in between, shows run in theatres, pubs, galleries, churches and car parks, and the city stays busy late into the night. It overlaps with the Adelaide Festival, Writers’ Week and WOMADelaide, a stretch that locals call “Mad March”. In 2023 it became the first festival in Australia to sell a million tickets.

Food

The festival hubs are also open-air food courts, with food trucks, bars and stalls from many cuisines, and many people come for the atmosphere and a meal as much as for a show. There is no traditional festival dish.

When

For about four weeks from mid-February to mid-March, at the end of the Australian summer:

  • 2027: 19 February – 21 March
  • 2028: not yet announced
  • 2029: not yet announced

Where

Across Adelaide, the capital of South Australia, on Kaurna land, with hubs in the East End parklands and events in suburbs and regional towns.

Adelaide Fringe on Wikipedia; Australasian Leisure Management, on the 2027 dates

Anzac Day

Observed: 25 April

Celebrated by day

Lest we forget The response said after the Ode of Remembrance

Anzac Day, on 25 April, is Australia’s and New Zealand’s national day of remembrance. It marks the landing of the Australian and New Zealand Army Corps at Gallipoli in 1915, and now honours everyone from both countries who has served and died in war and peacekeeping. It begins with dawn services in silence and continues with marches of veterans and their families.

Read more about Anzac Day

What it celebrates

It is a commemoration, not a celebration. On 25 April 1915, Australian and New Zealand troops landed on the Gallipoli peninsula in the Ottoman Empire, in an Allied campaign that failed and was abandoned eight months later; more than 8,700 Australians and 2,700 New Zealanders died there, along with many more British, French and Ottoman soldiers. Over time it became a day to remember all who have served, and in recent decades more attention has gone to people long left out of the story, including Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander and Māori service members and women.

How it’s celebrated

Before sunrise, people gather at war memorials across both countries, and at Gallipoli itself, for the dawn service: the hour recalls the landing, which began before dawn. The service includes the Last Post, a silence and the Ode of Remembrance, to which the crowd answers “Lest we forget”. Later, veterans, serving personnel and the descendants of those who served march through cities and towns, some wearing their relatives’ medals. People wear sprigs of rosemary, for remembrance, or red poppies.

Afternoons are quieter and more social: veterans and families meet at pubs and RSL clubs, where the coin game two-up is played, legal in most states only on this day.

Food

After dawn services, a “gunfire breakfast” of coffee, sometimes with a dash of rum, is served, as it was before battle. The best-known food is the Anzac biscuit, made with oats, golden syrup and coconut. The story that these biscuits were posted to soldiers in the trenches is mostly a later invention; the recipe as it is made today first appeared in the 1920s. The word “Anzac” is protected by law in Australia, and the government’s guidelines ask that the biscuits not be sold as “Anzac cookies”.

When

On 25 April every year, a public holiday in Australia and New Zealand.

  • 2027: Sunday 25 April
  • 2028: Tuesday 25 April
  • 2029: Wednesday 25 April

Where

At memorials in every city and town in Australia and New Zealand, at the Australian War Memorial in Canberra and the Auckland War Memorial Museum, and at Anzac Cove in Türkiye. The pin sits at the Australian War Memorial, and New Zealand is lit too.
